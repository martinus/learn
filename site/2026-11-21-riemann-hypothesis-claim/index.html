Developing story · status of 27 November 2026

A claimed proof of the Riemann Hypothesis

For 167 years, nobody could solve the most famous riddle in mathematics. On 20 November 2026 the mathematician Levent Alpöge said that he solved it together with Claude, an AI program. This page explains the riddle, the idea, and how sure we can be. You need no mathematics from school to read it.

Status: all checks passed, and a much shorter proof exists, but no complete text for people On 21 November, the communities behind the checking program Lean wrote in a joint statement that they have full confidence in the proof. All long checks have ended and passed, the last one on 25 November. New: on the same day, a team at Anthropic published a much shorter computer file that proves the same claims. It has about 113,000 lines in place of 17 million, and checking programs accept it too. A separate page explains how the proof became shorter. Jimmie Adriazola of Arizona State University wrote the first proof as a text of 287 pages (section 06). This text still uses steps that it does not prove, and no independent person has checked it. The Clay institute lists the riddle as active, not as solved. The author of the second proof, made by people only, wrote on 22 November that his paper is not ready and contains errors (section 07).

The riddle

Prime numbers appear without a visible pattern. The Riemann Hypothesis says that there is a strict order behind them.

The claim

A proof that this order is real. A person set the direction. An AI did much of the hard work.

The surprise

The proof came as a very large computer file that programs check step by step. A text for people now follows, and it is not complete.
01

The riddle: how are the prime numbers spread?

You can put 12 sweets into equal groups: 3 groups of 4, or 2 groups of 6. With 13 sweets this is not possible. Numbers such as 13 are prime numbers: 2, 3, 5, 7, 11, 13, 17. All other whole numbers are products of primes. This is why people call primes the building blocks of numbers.

Primes become less frequent when numbers get larger. Below 100, one number in four is a prime. Below one million, it is only one number in thirteen. But nobody can tell you by a simple rule where the next prime is.

There is a formula that predicts how many primes exist below a number X. The chart compares this prediction with the true count.

The Riemann Hypothesis says: the miss stays inside this zone forever, also for numbers with a thousand digits. The height of the zone is about the square root of X. For X = 1,000,000 the square root is 1,000, because 1,000 × 1,000 = 1,000,000.

A comparison: toss a coin one million times. You expect 500,000 heads. The true result is usually wrong by some hundreds, not by tens of thousands. Pure chance makes small errors of exactly this size. The hypothesis says that primes behave like these coin tosses. They look random, but no hidden force pushes them away from the prediction.

02

The hidden music of the primes

In 1859 the German mathematician Bernhard Riemann found out where the miss comes from. He used a formula with the name zeta function. You can think of it as a hidden sheet of music for the primes. The sheet contains special points, the zeros. Each zero is one tone.

The chart shows what the tones do. One tone alone is a simple wave. Add many tones, and the waves build a staircase. The staircase goes up exactly where the primes are.

Now the important question: where are these zeros? You can draw them on a map.

Why is the line so important? The position from left to right sets how loud a tone is. On the centre line, all tones stay quiet enough, and the miss from section 01 stays in the green zone. One zero to the right of the line is one tone that is too loud. Then the primes are less regular than everybody thinks.

03

Why it took 167 years, and what the new idea is

Mathematicians know a second, more simple world of numbers. It works like a model railway of the real number world: smaller, but with the same rules. This model world has its own primes and its own Riemann Hypothesis.

Solved

The model world

André Weil solved the riddle here in the 1940s. In 1969 Sergei Stepanov found a much shorter solution.

Open since 1859

The usual numbers 1, 2, 3, …

The plan for decades: copy the solution from the model world. It did not work, because one tool does not exist here.

The tool that was missing

The tool is a method to count one point more than one time. The pictures show the idea. A line can go through a point. It can also only touch the point, or stay flat on it. Mathematicians then count the point one, two or three times.

The new idea: a long chain

In the model world, the multiple count is easy to get. For the usual numbers, nobody knew how to get it. The note says that a tool from a very different area of mathematics can do the job. Its name is theta lift. Each use of the tool adds one more count. So you use it again and again, as links in a chain.

How long must the chain be? Claude proposed the square root of X. The author writes that this one line will astonish other mathematicians. His own comment in the note: “And yet, what else could it be?”

A limit of this page: the note does not explain the proof. This section gives the idea in the words of the note. The author also writes that he is not an expert in this part of mathematics. Here the AI knew more than he did. Section 06 shows the route in more detail, from a text that came later.

04

Six weeks, one person, one AI

The note lists 20 steps with dates. The work started on 6 October and ended on 16 November 2026.

Levent Alpöge is a mathematician at Anthropic, the company that makes Claude. He writes that he prepared for this riddle since his student days. He selected the direction and the ideas to combine.
Claude did much of the technical work, mainly at the end. The author writes that the AI was far ahead of him in that phase and that it made important proposals.
Together they wrote the proof in a form that a computer can check. The author says openly that he does not yet understand all parts of it.

How they worked

The note describes the method only in outline. It also says that Alpöge and Claude had worked together on many areas of mathematics in the months before. These are its main points about the method:

  • A solved model as a guide. In the model world of section 03, Alpöge saw two quite different ideas that worked together. One of them was new. He then looked for the matching situation in the usual numbers and led the work there. That was his part.
  • The AI brings the knowledge that the person lacks. The decisive steps use automorphic forms, an area of mathematics about functions with very many symmetries. Alpöge writes that he knows little about the advanced parts of this area. Claude did. The note says that the length of the chain in section 03 was only one of some tens of such proposals.
  • A computer check instead of a reader. They wrote the complete proof for Lean, the checking program of section 05, with no unproved assumptions. So anybody can check the result without understanding it. The note does not say whether Claude also used Lean during the search, for example to test single steps.
  • A messy path. Alpöge writes that the search went through great confusion. He expects that the proof contains strange choices that are not the best ones, and that much shorter proofs will follow. The list of 20 steps is not a record from the work: Claude reconstructed it afterwards. Where Claude is the expert, Alpöge repeats a summary by Claude, and he warns that it may not make sense.

What other problems can take from it

This page draws three lessons from the note. They are inferences of this page. No source states them as lessons.

  • Start from a version of the problem that is already solved. The person chose the direction by analogy with the model world. The AI then worked out the details in the hard version. This needs a person who knows the problem well. Alpöge prepared for it since his student days.
  • Let the AI bring the breadth. The decisive knowledge came from an area that the person did not master. An AI that knows many areas can connect them where one specialist cannot.
  • Use a strict checker, so that you need not trust the AI. Lean rejects every step without a reason. So it did not matter that no person could follow the work. In software, a compiler and a test suite play a similar part, but they are weaker: tests show that some cases work, not that all cases work.

The limits of these lessons: nobody can repeat the experiment, because the model, the prompts and the computing power are not public. And Gary Marcus wrote on 22 November that nobody has drawn general lessons yet about how to do mathematics with AI. He adds that methods whose results a program can check have helped mathematics more than other areas.

05

Can you trust a proof that no person has read?

The proof is a computer file with 175,187 parts and about 17 million lines. A checking program with the name Lean examines each step. Think of a very strict auditor. The auditor does not understand the business. But the auditor recalculates each line and accepts nothing without a reason.

Such a check can still go wrong in two ways. The file can prove a different, easier claim than the real riddle. Or it can contain a hidden shortcut: a step that it assumes but does not prove. So other people must examine these points. Andrew Sutherland of the university MIT keeps a public list of all checks.

The certificate. On 21 November, the people who build Lean and its mathematics library published a joint statement. They write that they have “full confidence” that the proof is correct, and they certify the claim and the proof. The two organizations that lead this work, the Lean FRO and the Mathlib Initiative, support the statement. The list shows the checks behind it, and the checks that came after it:

  • PassedThe check works on other computers.Dozens of people ran the file on their own computers. All got the same result, with zero errors.
  • PassedThe file proves the real riddle.Two tools compared the proved claim with the standard form of the riddle in the mathematics library of Lean. The two are identical.
  • PassedNo hidden shortcuts.The proof uses only the three basic assumptions that all of mathematics uses. Sutherland puts the chance of a trick at “approximately zero”.
  • PassedOther auditors come to the same result.Several separate checking programs accepted the proof. Some of them are written in other programming languages.
  • PassedA new build in a sealed environment.A separate computer built all parts again, closed off from outside influence. The result is identical to the published one, byte for byte.
  • PassedLean’s own checking program finished its long run.It examined the complete file in one run of 48 hours and accepted the proof on 22 November at 18:44 UTC. A variant of this checking program with the name “paranoid” needed 72 hours and accepted the proof on 23 November at 19:01 UTC.
  • PassedThe complete test in one run passed.A tool with the name Comparator does the main checks in one run: it compares the claim, looks for hidden shortcuts and starts two checking programs. After 64 hours, on 23 November at 09:10 UTC, it reported: “Your solution is okay!” Sutherland notes that this run did not build the files again. It used the files from his first build.
  • PassedA consequence without the zeta function.A known result from 1984, the criterion of Robin, connects the riddle with a simple rule. Take a whole number above 5,040 and add all numbers that divide it. The rule says that this sum stays below a certain limit. The riddle is true exactly if the rule is true for all these numbers. A separate project had proved this connection in Lean. Together with the new proof, the rule itself is now proved, and three checking programs accepted it. On 24 November, the separate project also passed the complete test on its own. The rule does not mention the zeta function from section 02. So this check does not depend on how the library defines that function.
  • PassedThe newer complete test passed too.It is a newer version of the complete test and ran since 20 November. On 25 November at 21:49 UTC it reported: “Your solution is okay!” It needed 122 hours and 45 minutes, because each of its checking programs used only one processor core. With this run, all checks on Sutherland’s list are complete.
  • Not yetPeople understand the proof.No independent mathematician has confirmed an explanation of why it works. After a first look, the well-known mathematician Terence Tao wrote that the proof seems to use two large areas of modern mathematics, and that it takes time to see how the parts fit together. First texts now show the route (section 06).
  • BegunA text that people can read.A text of 287 pages exists in a version of 24 November (section 06). It still uses steps that it does not prove, and no independent person has checked it. The note of Alpöge mentions a text of more than 250,000 pages, which he did not publish. The Next Web reports from his posts of 23 November: he meant this number as a joke. The pages exist, but they were never meant as a real write-up.
  • Not yetOfficial recognition, and the prize of 1 million dollars.The Clay institute has not recognized a solution. Its website lists the riddle as “Active”, in a group of its own next to the unsolved and the solved problems (read on 25 November). The Next Web reports that it stood with the unsolved problems before. The news page of the institute had no statement about the two proofs on that day. Its rules require a publication, then a waiting time of two years or more, and general acceptance by mathematicians.

The short answer: the proof passed the strongest test that exists for a proof of this type, and the experts for this test confirm it. So it is correct with very high probability. But checked is not the same as understood, and the mathematical community has not accepted it officially yet. The work to find out why the proof works has begun (section 06).

One limit stays. A checking program confirms that the claim follows from the definitions in its library. It cannot confirm that these definitions say what people mean. For this riddle, experts examined the definition before the proof appeared. The consequence about divisors makes this risk smaller: it depends on other definitions, not on the definition of the zeta function.

06

A first text for people: 287 pages

The computer file does not tell a person why the proof works. So mathematicians now write the proof as a text. This section is about the text by Jimmie Adriazola of Arizona State University. Its version of 24 November 2026 has 287 pages. The aim: a mathematician can read it from start to end and check it without Lean.

Adriazola did not translate the file line by line. The file has about 1.07 million named parts, and about 767,000 of them lead to the final result. The work started at the final result and followed backwards what it depends on. Where many parts of the file together express one idea, the text states that idea once. In one place, 631 proved statements of the file became eight. Adriazola used the AI programs Claude, GPT and Kimi for this work. Claude also wrote drafts of proofs.

The route in three steps

The note of four pages only named the idea. The text shows the route. Here are its three main steps, in the pictures of this page. Each step leaves out much detail.

  • One side is enough. A theorem of Edmund Landau from 1905, in a sharper form, makes the task smaller. Take a smoothed form of the miss from section 01. It is enough to show that it stays below a limit that is only a little above the upper edge of the zone. The lower edge needs no proof. And the limit must hold only on certain long stretches of numbers. Such stretches must exist as far out as you like.
  • Divisibility replaces the multiple count. Stepanov counted a point more than one time (section 03). For the usual numbers, the text counts how many times a prime divides a certain quantity. Each link of the chain adds more of this divisibility, at primes of one certain kind. In the final count, a large prime weighs more than a small one. It has the same weight as in the staircase of section 02, where large primes make higher steps.
  • Two errors pull against each other. Stepanov’s budget becomes an inequality about lattices, which are regular grids of points. Two errors remain in the result. One gets smaller when the chain gets longer. The other grows with the chain. The chart shows both for X = 1,000,000.

So the length of the chain is no free choice. A shorter chain leaves the first error too large, and a longer chain the second. The best balance is the square root of X. The total error then has about the size of the zone from section 01. Stepanov’s solution in the model world has the same balance.

What the text does not do yet

  • It is not complete. The text proves the riddle only together with a list of statements that it uses but does not prove. It calls each of them a hypothesis. This page counted 43 of them in the source of the text. All are in its second part, which builds the chain. Adriazola writes that the computer file proves each of them. So they are open only in the text for people.
  • No independent person has checked it. Adriazola writes: “I cannot guarantee that everything in this repository is correct.” The text also changes fast. It had fewer than 70 pages on 22 November, 225 pages on 23 November and 287 pages on 24 November. On 22 November, Adriazola wrote that the inner part of the work was complete. On 23 November, Adriazola corrected this two times. First: every part of the file had its place in the text, but not every statement had a written proof. Second: a new review found that the list of open statements was not complete. Adriazola writes that the reviews found no false statement.
  • One step goes beyond the published mathematics. One of the open statements is a limit for certain groups in number theory. Adriazola found that it implies progress on an old open question, the conjecture of Vandiver, beyond two published papers from 1999 and 2026. The computer file gets this limit from a statement that the published theorems of that area do not cover. The text does not claim this step. Adriazola names this area as a good place for specialists to start their check.
  • One part became shorter, without the checking program. For one limit, the computer file needs 21 steps. The text proposes a shorter argument. Lean has not checked this argument, and Adriazola asks other mathematicians to check it. If it holds, Adriazola sees a reason to think that the complete proof can become simpler. Alpöge expects the same in his note.

This is not the only text. Michael Rubinstein of the University of Waterloo wrote a guide of 265 pages for the Riemann Hypothesis alone, in a version of 22 November. It also rests on statements that it does not prove: thirteen hypotheses. The text of Adriazola names five more projects that explain, map or shorten the computer file.

What this changes for section 05: people can now read the route, and specialists can compare single steps with the published mathematics. This check has only started. A text that is complete, and that independent mathematicians accept, does not exist yet.

07

A second proof, made by people only

Some hours after the first news, a second proof became public. Ravi Vakil, the president of the American Mathematical Society, published a draft by the mathematician Alessandro Masullo. The paper has the name “The Music of the Primes”. Masullo writes that he worked on it for almost 12 years, without AI. He also writes that the draft is not complete, and that he was advised to publish it now.

A different idea

Masullo does not use the chain from section 03. He calculates one quantity in two ways: one time with the primes, and one time with the tones from section 02. He does this in a special setting. In this setting, only tones near the centre line can sound. He can make this zone as narrow as he wants. The two results must be equal. So there is no room for a tone that is away from the line. Masullo also writes that his method works for the usual numbers, but not in the model world. The first proof goes the opposite way: its idea comes from the model world.

The state of the second proof

  • Brian Conrad of Stanford, the former teacher of Masullo, examined the paper for months. He confirms this, but he does not say that the proof is correct. He says that the paper is in a large revision.
  • Masullo published his own statement on 22 November. He writes that the paper is not ready for publication. He also writes: “I am well aware of many bugs”, mainly in the last chapters. He planned to repair them during the next year. He writes that the decision to show the draft now was his own. The statement does not discuss the two objections below.
  • Two concrete objections are public, as the mathematician John Baez reports on 22 November. First: Eric Naslund examined the paper with the help of AI. A formula of the paper, tested near the prime 2, gives a different result than the paper says. Second: readers found a step on page 444 that uses a property which is apparently false in that situation.
  • The chapter that must get the final result from the main formula contains only placeholders.
  • Baez writes that the objections can still be repairable, or wrong. He does not say that the proof works, and he does not say that it failed.
  • The public draft stays as it is. Masullo keeps it on his own website without changes and plans a revised paper. Terence Tao wrote on 22 November that he expects no fast news about this paper, because the plan was one more year of work or more.
  • Volunteers started a project that writes this proof for the auditor Lean too, with the help of AI programs. Baez reports from the discussion about it: the project must wait until Masullo fills the gaps. Masullo writes that he hopes for such help when the time comes.

Can both proofs be correct? Yes. Two different routes can go to the same result. And a problem in the second proof says nothing about the riddle itself. It only means that this route is not confirmed. If both are correct, mathematicians have two different routes to study.

08

What this means for you

  • Your bank card stays safe. Encryption on the internet uses prime numbers. But the proof gives no method to break it. It only confirms what experts already assumed.
  • Nothing changes in daily life tomorrow. This is basic knowledge about numbers. Its effect is inside mathematics: many hundreds of results started with the words “if the Riemann Hypothesis is true”. With an accepted proof, these results become facts.
  • The method of work is the real news. An AI did a large part of the hardest work on the hardest known riddle. And machines confirmed the result before a person understood it. That is new.
  • Mathematicians have mixed feelings. Alpöge writes that he lost a guiding light of his life, and that he sees the start of something much larger for all of science. Masullo writes to his colleagues that human mathematicians are still here.
  • Not everybody sees a turning point. The author Gary Marcus, who often criticizes claims about AI, wrote on 22 November that “current AI could not have done it on its own”. He points out that an expert guided the AI, and that he has heard of no new method or idea from the proof so far.

Terms

Prime number
A whole number larger than 1 that you cannot write as a product of two smaller numbers. Read more: Wikipedia
Riemann Hypothesis
The guess of Riemann from 1859: every zero of the zeta function in the strip of the map lies on the centre line. This page uses a form that says the same: the miss of the prediction in section 01 stays small, about as large as the square root of X. Read more: claymath.org
Prediction formula (logarithmic integral)
Near a number t, about one number in ln t is a prime. ln is the natural logarithm: for t = 1,000,000 it is 13.8. The formula adds up these chances for all numbers up to X. Its name is li(X). Read more: Wikipedia
Zeta function
The formula of Riemann that connects all primes. In this page: the sheet of music. Read more: Wikipedia
Zero
A point where the zeta function has the value 0. In this page: one tone.
Centre line (critical line)
The vertical line at position ½ on the map of section 02. Mathematicians call it the critical line. The Riemann Hypothesis says that every zero in the strip lies on it.
Model world (function fields)
Number systems built from polynomials whose coefficients come from a finite set, instead of from the usual whole numbers. Mathematicians call them function fields over finite fields. André Weil proved the Riemann Hypothesis for them in the 1940s. Read more: Wikipedia
Multiple count (multiplicity)
A point where a curve only touches a line counts two times. A point where the curve stays flat on the line counts three times. Mathematicians call this number the multiplicity of the point. Read more: Wikipedia
Theta lift
A method from the theory of automorphic forms. With the help of a theta function, it turns an automorphic form for one group of symmetries into one for another group. In the note, each use of it adds one more count. Read more: Wikipedia
Automorphic forms
Functions that keep their value, up to a simple factor, under a large group of symmetries. They are a central area of modern number theory. Read more: Wikipedia
Computer proof (formal proof)
A proof in a language that a program can check. Each step must follow from earlier steps by fixed rules. The program accepts no step that it cannot check. Read more: Wikipedia
Lean
A program that checks a mathematical proof step by step. In this page: the auditor. Read more: lean-lang.org
Mathlib and the Mathlib Initiative
Mathlib is the large mathematics library of Lean, written by a community of mathematicians. The Mathlib Initiative is an organization that supports its development. Read more: mathlib-initiative.org
Lean FRO
The organization that leads the development of Lean. FRO stands for Focused Research Organization. Read more: lean-lang.org
Basic assumptions (axioms)
Statements that mathematics accepts without proof. Lean usually needs three: that two equivalent statements are equal, a rule for quotients, and the axiom of choice.
Criterion of Robin
Guy Robin showed in 1984: the Riemann Hypothesis is true exactly if every whole number n above 5,040 has this property. The sum of all divisors of n is less than about 1.781 · n · ln(ln n). Read more: Wikipedia
Clay institute and the Millennium Prize
The Clay Mathematics Institute in the USA offered a prize of 1 million dollars in 2000 for each of seven problems, the Millennium Prize Problems. The Riemann Hypothesis is one of them. Read more: claymath.org
MIT
Massachusetts Institute of Technology, a university in Cambridge near Boston, USA. Read more: mit.edu
UTC
Coordinated Universal Time, the reference time of the world. Austria is one hour ahead of it in winter (CET).
AI (artificial intelligence)
Here: a program that learned from very large amounts of text and can write text, program code and mathematics. Claude is such a program, made by the company Anthropic.
GPT and Kimi
AI programs like Claude. GPT comes from the company OpenAI, Kimi from the company Moonshot AI.
Lattice
A regular grid of points, like the corners of the squares on squared paper, also in more than two dimensions. Read more: Wikipedia
Conjecture of Vandiver
An open question of number theory, named after Harry Vandiver. It says that no prime p divides a certain number, the class number of a number system built from the p-th roots of 1. Read more: Wikipedia
Generalized Riemann Hypothesis
A larger version of the riddle for a full family of similar formulas. The two proofs claim to solve this version too. The checked computer file covers one important group of these formulas. Read more: Wikipedia
