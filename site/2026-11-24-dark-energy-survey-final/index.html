Is dark energy fading? The final DES result

The standard model of cosmology treats dark energy as a constant: every cubic metre of space holds the same amount of it, at all times. In May 2026 the Dark Energy Survey (DES) published the analysis that combines all of its measurements. Together with data from other experiments, a dark energy that changes over time fits better than a constant one, at 3.0σ. This page explains what was measured, how small the effect is, and how much weight 3σ can carry.

Status: a hint, no discovery The DES paper is a preprint of 26 May 2026, revised on 2 June 2026 and submitted to the journal Physical Review Letters. On 24 November 2026, arXiv listed no journal publication for it. The convention in physics for a discovery is 5σ. The next larger dataset, the full five-year survey of the DESI experiment, is expected in 2027.

The claim

In the best fit, dark energy is thinning out today, and it was denser a few billion years ago. A cosmological constant never changes.

The size

The best fit moves the distances to far galaxies by less than 2 %, as computed on this page. Supernova brightness must be calibrated to about 0.01 magnitudes to see that.

The strength

3.0σ with all data, 2.2σ from DES alone. A DES supernova paper translates a similar 3.2σ into odds of about 5 to 1.
01

What w says about dark energy

In 1998, two teams found from about 50 exploding stars that the expansion of the universe speeds up. The cause got the name dark energy. In the standard model it makes up about 70 % of the energy in the universe. The question of this page is whether its density changes while space expands.

Two numbers describe the expansion. The scale factor a is the size of the universe relative to today: a = 0.5 means that all distances between far galaxies were half as large. Astronomers measure the redshift z instead. Light that left a galaxy at scale factor a arrives with its wavelength stretched by 1 + z = 1/a. A larger z is further away and further in the past.

Each ingredient of the universe thins out in its own way while space grows. Physics describes this with one number per ingredient, the equation of state w, the ratio of pressure to energy density. The density then follows ρ ∝ a−3(1+w).

  • Matter: w = 0. The density falls with the volume, a−3.
  • Cosmological constant Λ: w = −1. The exponent is zero, so the density never changes. This is the dark energy of the standard model, which is called ΛCDM (Λ plus cold dark matter).
  • w > −1: the density falls while space grows. w < −1: the density rises. Physicists call this case “phantom”.

To test the constant, the DES paper lets w change with time in the simplest way, a straight line in a:

// CPL parametrisation
w(a) = w0 + wa * (1 - a)
// w0: the value today (a = 1)
// wa: the change towards the past
// Lambda-CDM: (w0, wa) = (-1, 0)

So the test is a comparison of nested models. The larger model has two more parameters, and the standard model is one point inside it. With all data combined, the DES paper finds w0 = −0.82 ± 0.05 and wa = −0.63 (+0.21, −0.18).

Integrating the density rule for this w(a) gives ρ(a)/ρ(1) = a−3(1+w0+wa) · e−3·wa·(1−a). The chart draws it for the DES fit.

This page computes the curve of the DES fit from the central values. No source states these densities. In this curve, dark energy grew until redshift 0.40, was then 9 % denser than today, and has thinned out since. Redshift 0.40 is about 4.4 billion years ago. This time is also computed here, with the ΛCDM parameters of the DES analysis of January 2026.

The curve is one of many that fit. The uncertainty of wa is about a third of its value, and the two parameters are correlated. The data constrain w best at redshift 0.32, where the paper finds w = −0.97 ± 0.02.

02

The effect: under 2 % in distance

No instrument measures the density of dark energy. What astronomers can measure is the distance to objects at a known redshift. The link between the two is the expansion rate H(z). In a flat universe with matter and dark energy, the Friedmann equation of general relativity gives it from the two densities. The distance is the path that light covers while space expands, an integral over 1/H.

// C++20. Flat universe: matter plus dark energy
// with w(a) = w0 + wa * (1 - a).
#include <cmath>

struct Model { double om, w0, wa; };  // om: matter share

// Dark energy density at scale factor a, today = 1.
double rho_de(const Model& m, double a) {
    return std::pow(a, -3 * (1 + m.w0 + m.wa))
         * std::exp(-3 * m.wa * (1 - a));
}

// Expansion rate H(z) / H0 (Friedmann equation).
double E(const Model& m, double z) {
    double a = 1 / (1 + z);
    return std::sqrt(m.om / (a * a * a)
                   + (1 - m.om) * rho_de(m, a));
}

// Distance to redshift z in units of c / H0 (Simpson).
double dist(const Model& m, double z, int n = 1000) {
    double h = z / n, s = 1 / E(m, 0) + 1 / E(m, z);
    for (int i = 1; i < n; ++i)
        s += (i % 2 ? 4 : 2) / E(m, i * h);
    return s * h / 3;
}

om is the share of matter in today’s energy density. The DES fit gives 0.311 ± 0.005. H0 is the expansion rate today. With the H0 of the fit, 67.4 km/s/Mpc, the unit c/H0 is about 4,400 megaparsecs. That is more than 14 billion light years. The code leaves out radiation, which matters only in the very early universe.

The call dist({0.31, -0.82, -0.63}, 0.5) / dist({0.31, -1, 0}, 0.5) returns 0.9836. With the same om and H0, an object at redshift 0.5 is 1.64 % closer in the DES fit than in the standard model. The chart shows this ratio for all redshifts that the surveys cover.

The whole signal is a shift of one to two percent that depends on redshift. Supernova surveys measure brightness in magnitudes, a logarithmic unit: 1 % in distance is 0.022 mag. The DES supernova team cites a 2024 paper by Efstathiou on what this demands. The brightness scale of nearby and distant supernovae must agree within 0.01 mag, across several telescopes. This page converts 0.01 mag to 0.46 % in distance, which is the band in the chart.

03

Three measurements, one camera

DES used one instrument: a 570-megapixel camera on the 4-metre Blanco telescope in Chile. It observed on 760 nights or half-nights between 15 August 2013 and 9 January 2019 and covered about 5,000 square degrees, one eighth of the sky. From these pictures, the collaboration built three different measurements. Two of them measure distance. The third measures how fast matter clumps together.

A two-point correlation function is a histogram over pairs. Take all pairs of galaxies, sort them into bins by their angle on the sky, and compare each bin with the count for random positions. For shapes, each pair contributes the product of its two measured distortions. The distortion of one galaxy by lensing is far smaller than its own irregular shape, so the signal shows only in the statistics of very many pairs. “3×2pt” stands for the three functions: shapes with shapes, positions with positions, and positions with shapes.

The fourth dataset in the combination comes from DESI, an instrument in Arizona. DESI takes a spectrum of each galaxy through one of 5,000 optical fibres and reads the redshift from it. DES is a camera and estimates the redshift from the colours in a few filters, which is less exact. The DESI data in this analysis are called DR2 and come from the first three years. Their BAO measurement uses more than 14 million galaxies and quasars. A quasar is the very bright centre of a distant galaxy.

04

No single probe carries the 3.0σ

For each combination of data, the paper states how far the standard model lies from the fit, in σ. Section 05 explains the unit. The 3×2pt data alone agree with a constant. The distance from the standard model grows as more datasets join.

Press “Leave one out”. Without the supernovae the result is 2.6σ, without the CMB 2.3σ, without any BAO data 2.4σ. The paper states that the best fit stays in the same region in every case: w0 > −1 and wa < 0. So no single dataset produces the preference alone. Without any one of them, at least 2.3σ remain.

The same table also gives reasons for caution:

  • More data did not raise the number. Supernovae, BAO and CMB without the DES 3×2pt data give 3.2σ. With 3×2pt, the constraint on w0 and wa is 10 % tighter, by the paper’s measure for the size of the uncertainty region. The result is 3.0σ.
  • A recalibration already cost one σ. With the first version of the DES supernova data, the combination with DESI and the CMB gave 4.2σ. In November 2025 the team published a new calibration, named DES-Dovekie. The same combination then gave 3.2σ.
  • A constant w other than −1 is not preferred. If w may differ from −1 but not change, the DES analysis of January 2026 finds w = −0.981 (+0.021, −0.022). It combines 3×2pt, the CMB, DESI BAO, supernovae and galaxy clusters. Only the model with a change in time fits better.

The DES team worked blind. It fixed all choices of the analysis before it looked at the cosmological result.

05

How strong is 3σ?

The fit produces a probability distribution over the plane of w0 and wa, from a sampler. The paper draws the contour of equal probability density that runs through the standard model at (−1, 0) and measures which fraction Δ of the distribution lies inside. It then reports the number of standard deviations at which a Gaussian holds the same fraction: n = √2 · erf−1(Δ). A second method, the difference in χ² between the best fits of the two models, gives nearly the same numbers.

3.0σ means that 99.73 % of the distribution lies inside the contour through the standard model. The χ² method gives the same 3.0σ and allows a second reading. If the standard model is true and all errors are understood, a fit that is this much better appears by chance in about 1 of 370 cases. Three things make the number weaker than it looks.

  • It holds only if all errors are in the model. A calibration error that nobody knows about is not part of the distribution. The step from 4.2σ to 3.2σ in section 04 came from such a correction and not from chance.
  • It does not compare the two models. The number says how surprising the data are under the standard model. A Bayesian comparison also charges the larger model for its two extra parameters. The DES-Dovekie paper gives this figure for its 3.2σ: odds of about 5 to 1 for the changing dark energy. By the usual thresholds, the paper calls that a weak preference.
  • The same question is tested again with each data release. A preprint by Kim, Mota and Tamosiunas (version of 22 October 2026) argues that this inflates the false-positive rate. For the DESI BAO data it calls the signal fragile. This page read only its abstract. The work looks at DESI data and not at the DES combination.

The DES paper itself speaks of a “weak preference for evolving dark energy”. The DESI paper of 2025 names 5σ as the usual threshold to establish new physics. That is a chance of 1 in 1.7 million.

06

What else could explain it

The sources discuss four explanations. None of them is ruled out on 24 November 2026.

Chance

For
A 3σ result appears by chance about once in 370 tries. The DESI paper names a statistical fluctuation as the simplest alternative.
Against
Every subset of the data that the DES paper tested points to the same region of w0 and wa.

Supernova calibration

For
The DESI paper says the supernova evidence rests on the comparison of the nearby ones (z < 0.1) with the rest. The nearby ones come from a mix of older surveys. One recalibration already removed one σ.
Against
Without any supernovae, the DES combination still gives 2.6σ. Two other supernova catalogues, combined with DESI and CMB data, give 3.2σ and 3.4σ after their own new analyses.

The early universe

For
The first stars ionised the gas in the universe, and free electrons scatter some of the CMB light. The optical depth τ measures how much. Sailer, Farren, Ferraro and White show that τ near 0.09 lowers the preference in DESI and CMB data from about 3σ to about 1.5σ.
Against
The Planck satellite measured τ = 0.0544 ± 0.0073. The authors put the gap at 3 to 5σ and see no sign of an error in the Planck data.

Dark energy changes

For
It fits all datasets at once, in every subset that the paper tested.
Against
The best fits cross w = −1. The DES paper states that a single ordinary scalar field cannot do this. It would need a coupling between dark energy and dark matter, or a change to gravity.

Quanta Magazine reports that some theorists doubt a changing dark energy for a further reason. Physics cannot yet explain why the energy of empty space has its small value. A value that also changes slowly would need a second explanation. Quanta also puts the τ idea into time: the gas would have to be ionised about 200 million years earlier than the roughly 700 million years after the Big Bang that the standard model assumes.

The line w0 + wa(1 − a) is a parametrisation and no physical theory. The DES-Dovekie paper notes that it can approximate many physical models. A preference for it says that some change over time fits better than none. It does not say that w follows this line.

07

What comes next

  1. 1998Two teams find the accelerated expansion from about 50 supernovae.
  2. Mar 2025DESI publishes its second data release (DR2). With the CMB, a changing dark energy is preferred at 3.1σ. With supernovae added, the value is between 2.8σ and 4.2σ, depending on the supernova catalogue.
  3. Nov 2025The DES supernova team publishes the DES-Dovekie recalibration. Its combination falls from 4.2σ to 3.2σ.
  4. Jan 2026DES publishes the 3×2pt analysis of all six years. Alone, it agrees with ΛCDM.
  5. 15 Apr 2026DESI completes its planned five-year survey with more than 47 million galaxies and quasars, against a plan of 34 million. It continues to observe through 2028.
  6. 26 May 2026DES publishes the combination of all its probes: 3.0σ with DESI and the CMB.
  7. 30 Jun 2026The Rubin Observatory in Chile starts its ten-year survey. Quanta Magazine reports that astronomers expect about 10 billion galaxies and millions of supernovae from it.
  8. 2027Expected: the first dark energy results from the full five-year DESI survey.

The DESI results of 2027 are the next test. They rest on five years of observation instead of three. This page infers what to watch for: if the effect is real, the significance should rise with the amount of data. If it stays near 3σ or falls, chance or a calibration error becomes more likely. The DESI paper notes that no supernova catalogue so far holds nearby and distant supernovae from one uniform programme. It expects such samples from the Rubin survey.

The DES paper announces a longer paper with the full analysis. A search on arXiv on 24 November 2026 found no such paper.

In practice, nothing changes for daily life or for engineering. The result concerns the expansion of the universe over billions of years. What an engineer can take from it is the method: blind analysis, three different measurements of one quantity, and a table that shows what happens when each input is removed.

08

Short glossary

Redshift z
The factor by which the expansion stretched the light of an object, minus 1. It serves as a measure of distance and of time into the past.
Equation of state w
Pressure divided by energy density. It fixes how the density of an ingredient changes while space expands.
ΛCDM
The standard model of cosmology: a cosmological constant Λ with w = −1, plus cold dark matter.
CPL parametrisation
w(a) = w0 + wa(1 − a), named after Chevallier, Polarski and Linder. The model with it is written w0waCDM.
Megaparsec (Mpc)
A unit of distance in astronomy: 3.26 million light years.
Magnitude (mag)
A logarithmic unit of brightness. A difference of 0.01 mag is 0.9 % in brightness or 0.46 % in distance.
BAO
Baryon acoustic oscillations: a preferred distance between galaxies of about 147 Mpc, left by sound waves in the early universe.
CMB
Cosmic microwave background: light from the time when the universe became transparent, at redshift 1,089.
3×2pt
Three two-point correlation functions of galaxy positions and galaxy shapes, analysed together.
DES and DESI
Two different projects. DES was a camera survey in Chile (2013 to 2019). DESI is a spectrograph in Arizona that has observed since 2021.
