Is 9/4 the exact exponent of matrix multiplication?

The school method multiplies two n×n matrices with n3 multiplications. Since 1969, algorithms have lowered this exponent, and many researchers hoped that it can come down to 2. The lowest exponent that algorithms can reach has the symbol ω (omega). In October 2026, an AI model of OpenAI proved that algorithms can come arbitrarily close to the exponent 9/4 = 2.25. A new paper of 111 pages from the same source, dated 24 November 2026, claims that no algorithm can do better, so ω would be exactly 9/4. This page explains what the exponent measures, how the proof works, and what a computer has checked.

Status: a claim of one company, not yet checked by others The paper appeared in a public repository of OpenAI on 25 November 2026 between 00:04 and 00:19 UTC, shortly after 01:00 CET. It is a preprint: no journal and no outside expert has reviewed it. OpenAI also published a proof for the proof checker Lean that covers the exponent itself. On 25 November, this page found no report of anyone outside OpenAI who had run this check or reviewed the paper. The other half of the claim, the algorithm with exponent 9/4, is in a better state: several researchers have confirmed it since October.

The question

How many arithmetic operations does the product of two n×n matrices need when n is large? The answer grows like a power of n. Until October 2026, the exponent was only known to lie between 2 and about 2.371.

The claim

The exponent is exactly 9/4. Algorithms that come arbitrarily close to it are known since October 2026. The new paper says that every algorithm needs at least n9/4/2 multiplications or divisions.

The check

No person outside OpenAI has confirmed the new paper. A proof of the same exponent exists as a file for a proof-checking program. The file is public, and no independent run is reported yet.
01

What the exponent ω measures

The cost model counts arithmetic operations on matrix entries: additions, subtractions, multiplications and divisions. Each operation costs 1, however large the numbers are. A program of such operations without branches is an arithmetic circuit. The entries come from a field, for example the rational numbers, the complex numbers, or the integers modulo a prime, which form a finite field.

The school method computes each of the n2 output entries as a dot product and needs n3 multiplications. In 1969 Volker Strassen found formulas that multiply two 2 × 2 matrices with 7 multiplications instead of 8. The formulas never swap the two factors of a product, so the entries may be matrices themselves. Split a large matrix into four blocks and apply the formulas again inside each block product. A matrix of side 2k then costs 7k multiplications, which is nlog27, about n2.807.

The same recursion works for any block scheme. Suppose a scheme multiplies two s×s matrices with r multiplications, and each multiplication takes a sum of entries of the first matrix times a sum of entries of the second. Such a scheme is a bilinear algorithm, and the smallest possible r is the rank of matrix multiplication for side s. The recursion gives:

ω≤logsr

Additions do not change the exponent, because their number grows more slowly in the recursion than the number of multiplications. ω is the infimum, the greatest lower limit, of all exponents that some algorithm reaches. No single algorithm has to reach it, so exact statements read “nω+ε operations for every ε>0”.

One lower bound is simple: the result has n2 entries, so ω≥2. Until this month, no larger lower bound for ω was known, as the authors of the 2024 record write in their paper. The chart shows what the exponents mean for the amount of work.

02

How the upper bound fell to 9/4

After Strassen, the record fell fast for two decades. In 1990, Don Coppersmith and Shmuel Winograd reached 2.376 with the laser method. This technique does not build an algorithm directly. It finds many independent matrix products inside high powers of a simpler object. Every later record refined this method. In 36 years these refinements lowered the bound by less than 0.005, to 2.371177 on 17 August 2026. The method also has a proven limit: applied to the object that these papers use, it cannot prove an exponent below 2.3078.

On 6 October 2026, OpenAI published a collection of more than 700 mathematical manuscripts. Its README says that an unnamed internal model produced them. Among them are three papers on ω. One refines the old method and reaches 2.371055 over every field. A second goes below 2.258 over the complex numbers. The third, 13 pages long and dated 2 October, proves ω≤9/4 over the complex numbers. Its proof compares matrix multiplication with the multiplication of polynomials.

This upper bound did not stay a claim of OpenAI. Within three weeks, other researchers rewrote the proof in their own terms:

  • Houxi Lü (12 October) replaced one construction, so that the proof no longer depends on the complex numbers.
  • Rafael Pass (20 October) gave a self-contained proof that constructs the algorithm, for integer matrices.
  • Alimzhan Amanov, Maxim van den Berg and Jeroen Zuiddam (21 October) proved the bound over every field. They also showed that the ingredients of this proof cannot give an exponent below 9/4.
  • Amir Shpilka (27 October) gave an elementary proof with explicit algorithms.

Two more expositions followed in November. All of these texts are preprints too. They come from different groups, and this page read their abstracts and not their proofs. After the release, the model continued with the opposite direction, a lower bound, as Sébastien Bubeck of OpenAI wrote on 25 November.

03

What the new paper claims

The main theorem says that ω=9/4 over every field. The new part is the lower bound, and the paper states it for every size, without hidden constants. For every n≥2, an arithmetic circuit that multiplies two n×n matrices contains at least n9/4/2 multiplication or division gates, rounded up. Additions, subtractions and multiplications by constants are free in this count. A bilinear algorithm needs at least n9/4 products. The factor 2 between the two bounds has a simple reason: the paper replaces each general multiplication or division gate by at most two products of the bilinear kind.

The table shows a limit of the new result. For sides from 3 to 33, the older bound is the higher one, as computed on this page. But the older bounds that the paper lists have the form of a constant times n2, and a constant factor says nothing about the exponent. The new bound is the first that grows faster than n2.

If the theorem holds, two beliefs fall. The first is the hope for ω=2, which an earlier record paper calls the “dream bound”. The second is a conjecture of Strassen about a class of tensors, which are arrays of numbers with three indices. It predicts the value n2 where the paper proves n9/4.

The theorem also contradicts a current preprint. In its first version, Zhi-Wei Sun claims a proof of ω=2. The OpenAI paper names the point of conflict: for side 256, Sun’s upper bound for a relaxed kind of rank is 217, and the new lower bound is 218. At most one of the two papers is right. This page cannot judge which.

04

The proof: a fast product would test lists too cheaply

The proof is indirect. It assumes an exponent below 9/4 and derives a contradiction in a different problem. Two objects carry the argument.

The machine model is a tensor network, a generalization of a Boolean circuit. Each vertex holds a table with one number for each setting of its bits. Here a vertex has at most three bits, so a table has at most 8 numbers. A wire joins two vertices and carries one bit. Bits without a partner are the exterior bits, the inputs. For one setting of the inputs, the network sums over all settings of the inner wires, and each term is the product of all table entries. A Boolean circuit is the special case in which exactly one setting of the inner wires contributes. The size of a network is the number of its vertices and wires.

The test problem is list distinctness. The input is a list of 2w words with 2w bits each. The output is 1 if all words differ and 0 if a word repeats. The obvious solution sorts the list and compares neighbours. A sorting network from 1968 does this with about w2 gates per input bit.

The paper then proves two statements that cannot both hold:

  • A floor (Theorem 2.2). A network may test many independent lists at once and share work between them. It still needs more than wσ vertices and wires per input bit. Here σ (sigma) is a fixed positive constant. The statement holds for all large w and for up to 2w lists.
  • A compression (Theorem 2.1). Assume that ω<9/4 over a finite field. Then every network of moderate size can be rebuilt as one network that evaluates several copies of it on separate inputs, at a lower cost per input bit.

The compression has this form. Let x be the cost of a network per exterior bit, its size divided by the number of its exterior bits, and let h be the logarithm of its number of exterior bits, which is about w for the list test. One round of compression gives, with two positive constants γ (gamma) and η (eta):

xj+1≤h−γxj+hη

The first term shrinks the network by a power γ of the logarithm. The second term is a fee per exterior bit. The paper may choose its power η as small as it likes, and γ>0 then depends on η. The result is again a network on separate bits, so the round can repeat. Start with the sorting network at x0≈w2 and choose η<σ. After more than 2/γ rounds, the cost per input bit is about wη. This is below the floor wσ, a contradiction. So ω≥9/4 over every finite field.

One step leads from finite fields to all fields. An algorithm that beats 9/4 over any field can be turned into one fixed bilinear algorithm with finitely many coefficients. The paper shows that these coefficients can be mapped into a finite field, and the algorithm stays correct there.

The two theorems carry very different weight. The proof of the compression fills about 90 of the 111 pages, and this page does not explain it. One of its building blocks is a sorting result. Under the assumption ω<9/4, a network sorts 2u records of about u bits at a cost of up per record, with a power p below 2. Sorting is the tool that evaluates many copies of one table at once. The proof of the floor takes three pages, because it rests on two other papers of the same release. One of them, on lower bounds for arithmetic circuits, makes claims of at least the same size. So the paper is only as strong as these companions.

05

Where the number 9/4 comes from

The proof measures algorithms with prices. First, matrix multiplication becomes one object, a tensor. It has three sites: A and B for the two inputs and C for the output. The input entry aij, the input entry bjk and the output entry cik belong together. So each of the three indices is shared by exactly two sites: j by A and B, k by B and C, i by C and A.

A price is a function that gives each tensor a real number and obeys three rules. Combining two independent tensors adds their prices. A choice among q alternatives that all sites know costs a fixed rate times logq. And the price never rises when each site transforms only its own part with a linear map. Strassen introduced such functions in 1988. They fit the problem, because an algorithm with r products is exactly such a transformation. It starts from one label with r values that all three sites see, the number of the product, and each site applies its own linear map.

Each price function defines a geometry. An index with q values that two sites share is a wire. Its price is d·logq, and the factor d behaves like a distance between the two sites. A label that all three sites share costs D·logr, with the shared-label rate D. Matrix multiplication is three wires of n values each, and the algorithm builds it from the shared label. The third rule gives:

(dAB+dBC+dCA)·logn≤D·logr

Solved for r, every algorithm needs at least nP/D products, where P is the perimeter of the triangle ABC. Each price function therefore proves ω≥P/D. The question is which geometries exist.

A companion paper constructs prices in which the sites are points of ordinary Euclidean space. There, the rate D of three sites is half the perimeter of their triangle. The ratio is 2, which gives only the old bound ω≥2. The value 9/4 needs a space that branches, a tripod: three arms that meet in one centre, with the sites at the tips. The way from one tip to another leads through the centre. For arms of the lengths a1, a2, a3, the paper proves (Proposition 8.1) that the rate is at least D*. Here S is the sum of the arm lengths, and R=1/a1+1/a2+1/a3 is the sum of their reciprocals:

D*=S−1R,S=a1+a2+a3

With three arms of length 1, any two tips are 2 apart, so the perimeter is 6. The formula gives D*=3−1/3=8/3. If a price function has such a tripod with a rate of exactly 8/3, it proves ω≥6/(8/3)=9/4.

The paper does not simply exhibit this tripod. It argues by two cases. If a price function with the tripod exists, the bound 9/4 follows as shown. If none exists, a whole family of tensors becomes rigid: the cheapest placement of its sites cannot move when the parameters of the family change. The paper turns this rigidity into the sorting result and the compression of section 04, and these contradict the floor for list distinctness. Both cases end at ω≥9/4.

06

What a computer has checked

A proof of 111 pages by an AI model, resting on two more such papers, is hard to trust on reading alone. OpenAI therefore published a second object: a proof in Lean, a programming language in which a proof is a program. A small core of Lean, the kernel, checks every step. The statement to be proved stands in a separate file of 136 lines that needs only Mathlib, the standard mathematics library of Lean. Shortened, it reads:

-- abridged from MatrixExponent.lean
inductive Gate (F Input Register) where
  | constant : F → Gate ..      -- cost 0
  | input : Input → Gate ..     -- cost 0
  | add : Register → Register → Gate ..
  | sub : Register → Register → Gate ..
  | mul : Register → Register → Gate ..
                                -- cost 1 each

-- every output is the polynomial of
-- the matrix product
def FormalCorrect (P) : Prop :=
  ∀ i k, registerPolynomial P.program
      (P.output i k)
    = ∑ j, X (inl (i, j))
           * X (inr (j, k))

-- infimum of the exponents τ with
-- cost ≤ C · N^(τ+ε) for all sizes N
def formalOmega (F) (w12 w23 w31 : ℝ) :=
  sInf {τ | FormalAdmissibleExponent
              F w12 w23 w31 τ}

theorem square_exponent
    (F : Type*) [Field F] :
    formalOmega F 1 1 1 = (9 : ℝ) / 4

The theorem is an equation, so it contains both directions: algorithms with exponent 9/4 exist, and none is better. The tool Comparator of the Lean project is made for this situation, in which the reader does not trust the author of a proof. It confirms three things: the proof file proves exactly the statement of the short file, the Lean kernel accepts it, and it uses no axioms beyond three standard ones. A reader who runs it has to trust the kernel, the definitions of Mathlib, the 136 lines and their own computer, and no longer the 111 pages.

The Lean proof is large. The folder with the final theorem and the folder for the matrix exponent hold 1,395 files with 347,203 lines, and they import code from further folders. This page counted them in the repository state of 25 November 2026, 00:18 UTC. A text search found neither sorry, the Lean keyword for a missing proof, nor an added axiom in these files. Such a search is no check.

The Lean proof and the paper are two separate objects. A valid Lean proof does not show that each step of the paper is right, and OpenAI does not say that the Lean proof follows the paper. The two also differ in one place. For products of an n×nβ matrix with an nβ×n matrix, the paper asks up to which power β (beta) the exponent 2 can be reached. It proves that this limit lies between 1/2 and 2/3. The Lean scope document states the exact value 1/2.

The history of the collection is a reason for care. Its README calls the manuscripts “raw research data” and says that results without a Lean proof may contain errors. On 7 October 2026, one day after the first release, OpenAI withdrew three manuscripts because of a sign error and revised 14 others. According to the README, about 42 % of the main results have a Lean proof.

First reactions exist, and they are no reviews. The computer scientist Scott Aaronson answered with astonishment when readers posted the result in his blog on 24 November, and he announced a longer text.

07

What changes in practice

For code, nothing changes. Numerical libraries multiply matrices with the cubic method, tuned for caches and vector instructions. Strassen’s formulas pay off only for large matrices. These two statements come from the general knowledge of the model that wrote this page, and no source of this page covers them. The algorithms with exponent 9/4 are far from use. The October paper says that its proof “does not specify a competitive finite matrix size”. In Shpilka’s explicit version, an algorithm with exponent 9/4+ε starts from a base matrix whose side can be as large as 2(1/ε)c, with a constant c that the abstract does not give.

The lower bound also covers less than a headline suggests:

  • It counts arithmetic operations. It says nothing about the running time on real hardware.
  • It holds for arithmetic circuits that are correct as formulas, for all inputs at once. An algorithm that looks at the bits of the numbers, or one that may return an approximate result, is outside this model. This page infers this from the definition of the model. The paper does not discuss it.
  • For the matrix sizes of real programs, the bound itself is weak. At n=1,024 it demands 5.9 million products, and Strassen’s recursion uses 282 million.

The change is in theory. Many algorithms for graphs and linear algebra have running times that contain ω, because their main step is a matrix product. One example from the follow-up papers: with the new upper bounds, Przemysław Uznański gets n2.5 for shortest paths between all pairs of vertices in a directed graph without weights. If the lower bound holds, a running time of nω cannot improve through a faster matrix product. This last sentence is an inference of this page.

Four things will decide the claim: an independent Comparator run of the Lean proof, a review of the paper by specialists, the fate of the two companion papers, and the conflict with Sun’s preprint.

08

Terms

ω (omega, the exponent of matrix multiplication)
The smallest number such that two n×n matrices can be multiplied with nω+ε arithmetic operations, for every ε>0. It lies between 2 and 3. Wikipedia: Computational complexity of matrix multiplication
Field
A set of numbers with addition, subtraction, multiplication and division by everything except zero. Examples are the rational, real and complex numbers, and the integers modulo a prime. The last kind is a finite field.
Arithmetic circuit
A program without branches and loops. Each step adds, subtracts, multiplies or divides two earlier values, inputs or constants. Its cost is the number of steps. It must be correct as a formula in the input variables, for all inputs at once.
Bilinear algorithm
A scheme for a matrix product in which each multiplication takes a sum of entries of the first matrix times a sum of entries of the second. The outputs are sums of these products. Strassen’s seven formulas are one.
Rank r of matrix multiplication
The smallest number of products in a bilinear algorithm for matrices of a given side. For side 2 it is 7. The exponent follows from the ranks: ω is the infimum of logsr over all sides s.
Tensor
An array of numbers with three or more indices. The matrix multiplication tensor has an entry 1 for each triple of an entry of the first input, an entry of the second input and the output entry that their product feeds. All other entries are 0. Wikipedia: Matrix multiplication algorithm
Laser method
A technique of Strassen from 1986. It does not search for a matrix multiplication algorithm directly. It takes a tensor that is cheap to compute and shows that high powers of it contain many independent matrix products.
Preprint
A scientific paper that its authors publish themselves, before any journal has had it reviewed by independent experts.
Tensor network
A graph in which each vertex holds a small table of numbers and each wire carries a bit shared by two vertices. Its value for given inputs is the sum, over all settings of the inner wires, of the product of the selected table entries. A Boolean circuit is a special case.
Exterior bit
A bit of a tensor network that belongs to only one vertex. The exterior bits are the inputs of the network.
List distinctness
The test problem of the proof. Given 2w words of 2w bits each, return 1 if all words differ and 0 otherwise.
xj (size per exterior bit)
The number of vertices and wires of a tensor network, divided by the number of its exterior bits, after j rounds of compression.
h
The logarithm to base 2 of the number of exterior bits of a network, rounded up. For the list test it is about w, half the length of a word.
γ (gamma)
The power of h by which one round of compression shrinks a network. The paper proves that a positive value exists if ω<9/4, and gives no number.
η (eta)
The power of h that one round of compression charges per exterior bit. It can be chosen as small as needed, and must be positive.
σ (sigma)
The power of w in the floor for list distinctness. It is a fixed positive constant that depends only on the field. In one of the two cases of the proof, every value below 1/2 works.
Price
A number that a fixed rule gives to each tensor. Prices add when independent tensors are combined, and they never rise when each site applies a linear map to its own part. The paper uses the logarithm of what the literature calls a spectral point.
Site
One of the parties that a tensor connects. Matrix multiplication has three sites: the first input, the second input and the output.
Wire and distance d
A wire is an index that exactly two sites share. A price function charges d·logq for a wire with q values. The factor d obeys the triangle inequality, so it is a distance between the two sites.
Shared-label rate D
The price factor of a label that all three sites see. Such a label with r values costs D·logr. D* is the lowest value that the paper allows on a tripod.
Tripod with arms a1, a2, a3
A space made of three line segments that meet in one centre. The distance between two tips is the sum of their two arm lengths, because every path leads through the centre.
Lean
A programming language and proof checker. A proof is a program, and a small kernel checks that each step follows from the definitions and the earlier steps. lean-lang.org
Mathlib
The community library of mathematics for Lean. It defines fields, polynomials, real numbers and the infimum that the statement uses. Mathlib on GitHub
Comparator
A tool of the Lean project for proofs from an author whom the reader does not trust. It checks in a sandbox that a proof file proves the same statement as a short challenge file, that the kernel accepts it, and that it uses only the permitted axioms. Comparator on GitHub
