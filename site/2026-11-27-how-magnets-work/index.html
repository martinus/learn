Physics · status of 27 November 2026

How do magnets work?

A magnet pulls on iron without touching it and without a power supply. The source is the electron. Each electron is a tiny magnet of fixed strength, through a property called spin. In most materials the electrons cancel each other. In iron and a few other materials, a quantum effect of the electric force aligns them, and their sum is a magnetic field that a hand can feel. This page follows the chain from one electron to the neodymium magnet in a motor, and it says how each step is known.

The source

Every electron is a magnet of the same fixed strength. A permanent magnet is a material that keeps a large share of its electrons pointing the same way.

The glue

Magnetic forces between atoms are about 2,600 times too weak to keep iron ordered up to its limit of 770 °C, as computed on this page. The order comes from the electric repulsion between electrons and a rule of quantum mechanics.

The limit

The unit of field strength is the tesla. Iron with all its electron magnets aligned gives 2.2 tesla, as computed on this page. The strongest common magnets contain the metal neodymium and reach 1.45 tesla, about 29,000 times the field of the Earth in Vienna.
01

A magnet is a current around its rim

Every magnetic field has one of two sources: an electric current, or the built-in magnetic moment of a particle such as the electron. Section 02 covers the second source. The first is older knowledge. In 1820, Hans Christian Ørsted saw a compass needle turn next to a wire that carried a current. André-Marie Ampère concluded that a magnet contains many small circular currents.

Ampère’s picture gives the right field. Think of a magnet as a stack of small current loops that all turn the same way. Inside, the currents of neighbouring loops run in opposite directions and cancel. At the surface nothing cancels them, so one current remains, and it runs around the rim of the magnet. Outside, a cylinder magnet has the field of a coil of the same shape. The strength of the rim current follows from the magnetisation M, the magnetic moment per volume:

M=Brμ0

Br is the remanence, the field that the material keeps without any outside field. μ0 is the magnetic constant. The unit of M is ampere per metre: it is the rim current for each metre of magnet length. N52, a strong grade of neodymium magnet, has Br=1.45 tesla (T), the unit of field strength, in the data sheet of Arnold Magnetic Technologies. This page computes 1.15 million ampere per metre from it. A disc of this material that is 10 mm high acts like one turn of wire with 11,500 A. This current is no flow of electrons through the metal. It is the sum of the electron states of section 02, so it needs no power and makes no heat.

On the axis of a cylinder with the length L and the radius R, the field B at the centre of the pole face is:

B=Br2·LL2+R2

The formula is the textbook result for the field of the rim current. It has a practical consequence. A thin disc has a weak field at its face, however strong its material is. A long rod approaches Br/2 at this point and never passes it. Far away, the field of every magnet falls with the cube of the distance.

With the slider at 10 mm, the field is 0.65 T at the pole face, 55 mT at a distance of 10 mm and 1.1 mT at 50 mm. This is still 22 times the field of the Earth in Vienna. The World Magnetic Model gives 49 µT there for 27 November 2026.

Why a moving charge makes a magnetic field at all is a result of special relativity. A force that one observer calls magnetic is an electric force for an observer who moves along with the charge. Magnetism and electricity are one force, seen from different frames of reference. This paragraph is textbook physics from the knowledge of the model that wrote this page.

02

The current is the spin of electrons

An electron has a charge, a mass and a third fixed property, the spin. Spin behaves like a rotation of fixed size, and it makes the electron a magnet. The magnetic moment of the electron is almost exactly one Bohr magneton:

μB=eℏ2me=9.274×10−24J/T

e is the charge of the electron, ℏ is the reduced Planck constant and me is the mass of the electron. The value is the recommended value of 2022 from CODATA, the international committee for the constants of physics. The unit joule per tesla gives the energy that it costs to turn the moment against a field. In a field of 1 T, turning one electron over costs 0.12 meV, thousandths of an electronvolt. The thermal energy at room temperature is 25 meV, about 200 times more. A field of 1 T is therefore far too weak to hold single electrons in line against heat. Both numbers are computed on this page.

The moment of the electron is among the best-measured numbers in physics. A group at Northwestern University and Harvard held one single electron in a magnetic trap. In 2023 it reported a moment of 1.001 159 652 180 59 Bohr magnetons, with an uncertainty of 0.13 parts in a trillion. The first direct sign was older. In 1922, Otto Stern and Walther Gerlach sent a beam of atoms through a magnetic field. The experiment showed that the magnetic moment of an atom takes only certain fixed values.

Classical physics cannot build a magnet from moving charges in a material. Niels Bohr showed this in his thesis of 1911, and J. H. van Leeuwen proved it independently in 1919. If the charges follow classical mechanics and are in thermal equilibrium, their magnetic effects cancel exactly. In his Nobel lecture of 1977, John Van Vleck summed up the result: there should be “no magnetism at all”. A magnet on a refrigerator is therefore a quantum effect at room temperature, at a size that a hand can hold.

Most materials are still not magnetic, because their electrons cancel in pairs. The Pauli principle allows two electrons in one orbital only if their spins are opposite, and such a pair has no net moment. The page on what an atom is made of explains orbitals and shells. A filled shell contributes nothing. Only a partly filled shell can leave electrons without a partner. Hund’s rule of 1925 says how such a shell fills. The electrons first take separate orbitals with parallel spins, and they pair up only when no empty orbital is left. Section 03 gives the reason.

The ten elements from scandium to zinc fill the 3d shell, which has five orbitals. The figure shows their free atoms.

Chromium has six unpaired electrons and manganese has five. Neither metal is a ferromagnet, the kind of material that permanent magnets are made of. In this row, only iron, cobalt and nickel are ferromagnets. Unpaired electrons make each atom a magnet. A piece of metal is a magnet only if its atoms agree on one direction. Where the atomic moments point in random directions, the material is a paramagnet: a field aligns its moments a little, and the effect ends with the field.

The motion of an electron around the nucleus is a current as well, and it adds a second moment. This page follows the spin only, which gives most of the moment in iron. This sentence rests on the knowledge of the model that wrote this page.

03

An electric force aligns the spins

The first candidate for the aligning force is the magnetic force between neighbouring atoms. It is far too weak. Two neighbouring atoms in iron are 0.248 nm apart, and each carries a moment of about 2.2 μB. The magnetic energy between two such moments is at most 0.034 meV, as computed on this page with the formula for two dipoles. This is the thermal energy at 0.4 K, a temperature just above absolute zero. Iron keeps its magnetic order up to 1,043 K, its Curie temperature. The force that aligns the spins must be about 2,600 times stronger. Pierre Weiss met this problem in 1907. Van Vleck recounts that the model of Weiss needed an internal field of the order of 1,000 times the magnetisation, far above what classical electrodynamics gives.

Werner Heisenberg found the source in 1928. It is the exchange interaction, and it is electric. Two facts combine. First, electrons repel each other, and the energy of this repulsion is high when they are close. Second, the Pauli principle is a rule for the wave function of two electrons: it must change its sign when the two electrons swap. For two electrons with parallel spins, this rule forces the wave function to zero wherever both would be at the same place. Electrons with parallel spins therefore keep apart, and their repulsion energy is lower. Electrons with opposite spins have no such rule and come close. The direction of the spins changes the electric energy, although no magnetic force acts.

The figure shows the rule in the simplest case with an exact solution: two electrons in a one-dimensional box, in two different orbitals.

The energy that parallel spins save is the exchange energy. Its size in iron is of the order of kB times the Curie temperature, where kB is the Boltzmann constant. This is 90 meV, about 2,600 times the magnetic energy above. No source states this number. It is an estimate of this page.

Parallel spins also have a price. Electrons that keep apart need wave functions with more curvature, and this means more energy of motion. The material decides which side wins:

  • Ferromagnet: parallel wins, and the moments of the atoms add up. Iron, cobalt and nickel are the examples in the row of section 02.
  • Antiferromagnet: neighbouring moments point in opposite directions, and the piece has no field outside. Louis Néel described this order in 1932.
  • Ferrimagnet: the opposite moments have unequal sizes, so a net moment remains. Néel introduced this case later.
  • Altermagnet: a class that physicists have studied in the last few years. The neighbouring spins cancel as in an antiferromagnet, and the material still shares properties with a ferromagnet. In June 2026, the Institut Laue-Langevin in Grenoble reported neutron measurements on manganese telluride that confirm a prediction for this class.

For a metal, this picture is a simplification. In iron, the electrons that carry the moment also move through the crystal. A theory with moments fixed to the atoms (Heisenberg) and a theory with moving electrons (Edmund Stoner, 1936) each explain a part. A history of the field by Navinder Singh (preprint of 2018) notes that theory alone has not proved the sign of the exchange for any ferromagnetic material. Numerical methods come closer. A study by Pavizhakumari, Skovhus and Olsen (2025) computes Curie temperatures between 680 and 1,128 K for iron, depending on the approximation. The measured value is 1,043 K.

04

Heat ends the order at the Curie point

The exchange interaction favours order, and heat favours disorder. The standard model for this contest is the Ising model. Wilhelm Lenz proposed it in 1920, and his student Ernst Ising solved it for a chain of spins in 1925. Each site i of a grid holds a spin si that is +1 or −1. Each pair of neighbours with equal spins lowers the energy E by J, the exchange energy of one pair:

E=−J∑⟨i,j⟩sisj

The sum runs over all pairs of neighbours. At the temperature T, statistical mechanics gives a state with the energy E a probability that is proportional to e−E/kBT. The Metropolis algorithm of 1953 draws states with this probability. It visits each spin and flips it if the flip does not raise the energy. It accepts a flip that raises the energy by ΔE with the probability e−ΔE/kBT.

// C++20. One sweep over an N x N grid.
// s[i] is +1 or -1. T is k_B * T / J.
void sweep(std::vector<int>& s, int N,
           double T, Rng& rng) {
  // a flip costs dE = 2 * s * (neighbours)
  const double p4 = std::exp(-4.0 / T);
  const double p8 = std::exp(-8.0 / T);
  for (int y = 0; y < N; ++y)
    for (int x = 0; x < N; ++x) {
      int up = ((y + N - 1) % N) * N + x;
      int dn = ((y + 1) % N) * N + x;
      int lf = y * N + (x + N - 1) % N;
      int rt = y * N + (x + 1) % N;
      int i = y * N + x;
      int dE = 2 * s[i]
          * (s[up] + s[dn] + s[lf] + s[rt]);
      // downhill always, uphill by chance
      if (dE <= 0 ||
          rng.uniform() < (dE == 4 ? p4 : p8))
        s[i] = -s[i];
    }
}

For a square grid, Lars Onsager solved the model exactly in 1944. Order exists only below a sharp temperature, the Curie temperature TC:

kBTC=2Jln(1+2)≈2.269J

Below TC, the average spin of an infinite grid is the eighth root of 1−1/sinh4(2J/kBT). Chen Ning Yang published the proof in 1952. The figure runs the code above on a grid of 64 × 64 spins and compares the result with this formula.

The simulation follows the exact curve at low temperature. Near TC it lies above the curve, because a grid of 4,096 spins keeps a majority by chance where an infinite grid has none. Above TC, a ferromagnet is a paramagnet.

The table in the study of Pavizhakumari and others lists the measured Curie temperatures of the three metals: 1,043 K (770 °C) for iron, 1,388 K (1,115 °C) for cobalt and 627 K (354 °C) for nickel. Nd₂Fe₁₄B, the compound in neodymium magnets, has only 586 K (313 °C), measured on single crystals by Susner and others (preprint of 2015).

The model is a toy. Its spins point only up or down, and its grid is flat. Real spins point in any direction, and real magnets have three dimensions. The sharp transition is common to all of them.

05

Why a nail is no magnet

At room temperature, iron is far below its Curie temperature, so its spins are aligned. A nail still attracts nothing. The alignment holds only inside small regions, the domains, and the domains point in different directions. Felix Bloch described the boundaries between domains in 1932. Lev Landau and Evgeny Lifshitz explained the pattern of the domains in 1935.

Domains save energy. A piece that is magnetised in one direction fills the space around it with a field, and this field holds energy. If the piece splits into domains with opposite directions, the field lines close inside the material and the outside field nearly vanishes. Each boundary, a domain wall, costs energy, because the spins inside the wall are not parallel to their neighbours. The material settles in the pattern with the lowest total energy.

An outside field moves the walls. The domains that point along the field grow, and the nail is a magnet as long as the field lasts. This is how a magnet attracts iron: it magnetises the iron and then pulls it towards the stronger field. In pure iron the walls move easily in both directions, so the nail loses its magnetisation when the magnet is gone. This paragraph and the one before it are textbook physics from the knowledge of the model.

A permanent magnet must stop its walls from forming and moving. Its material needs a strong magnetic anisotropy: the crystal has one direction, the easy axis, along which the magnetisation prefers to lie. Turning the magnetisation away from the axis costs the energy K for each unit of volume. Nd₂Fe₁₄B has K=4.9 MJ/m³. Its saturation magnetisation Ms, the magnetisation with all moments aligned, corresponds to a field of 1.61 T. Both values are from a review by Fischbacher and others (2018), from a group at the Danube University Krems in Lower Austria. The second need is the structure. A sintered magnet consists of many small crystal grains. The review names small grains and thin non-magnetic layers between them as essential for a magnet that resists reversal.

The simplest model of one grain is from Edmund Stoner and Erich Wohlfarth (1948). The grain is a single domain. An outside field H against its magnetisation tries to turn it. Like the magnetisation, H is counted in ampere per metre, and multiplied by μ0 it is a value in tesla. The magnetisation flips when the field passes a threshold, and it does not flip back when the field is gone. The scale of the threshold is the anisotropy field:

HK=2Kμ0Ms

For Nd₂Fe₁₄B this is 7.65 T, as computed on this page from the two values above. The figure applies a field to 11 such grains. Their easy axes lie between 2.5 and 27.5 degrees away from the direction of the field.

The loop is called hysteresis, and it is the memory of a magnet. The field that brings the magnetisation to zero is the coercivity. In this model it is 0.62 HK.

Real magnets stay far below the model. The data sheet of N52 gives a coercivity of at least 875 kA/m. Multiplied by μ0, this is 1.1 T, or 14 % of the anisotropy field, as computed on this page. Fischbacher and others write that the highest possible coercivity is never reached in experiments, and that this gap has the name Brown’s paradox. In their simulations, defects and misaligned grains lower it. Heat lowers it further. The data sheet gives a loss of 0.62 % of the coercivity for each degree, measured between 20 and 60 °C. Magnets for motors therefore replace a part of the neodymium by dysprosium. With about 10 weight percent of it, a magnet keeps a coercivity above 1 T at 450 K (177 °C), according to the same review.

06

How strong a magnet can get

The field of a permanent magnet has a ceiling: the number of unpaired electrons in a volume. Iron has 8.49 × 1028 atoms in a cubic metre, as computed on this page from its density and its atomic mass. The study of Pavizhakumari and others computes a spin of 1.10 ℏ for each atom, which is a moment of 2.2 μB. With all moments aligned, the magnetisation is 1.73 million ampere per metre. Multiplied by μ0, this is 2.2 T. Iron cannot give more. Nd₂Fe₁₄B reaches 1.61 T, and the grade N52 keeps 1.45 T of it as remanence.

Stronger fields need a current in a coil. A copper coil turns its electric power into heat, so strong magnets use superconductors. A typical MRI scanner of a hospital works at 1.5 to 3 T, according to MagLab, the National High Magnetic Field Laboratory of the United States. The bending magnets of the LHC, the particle accelerator of CERN near Geneva, make 8.3 T with a current of 11,080 A. In January 2026, the Chinese Academy of Sciences reported 35.6 T for a magnet in Beijing that consists only of superconductors. The strongest continuous fields come from hybrid magnets, a coil of normal metal inside a superconducting coil. A hybrid magnet in Hefei in China reached 45.22 T in August 2022. MagLab had held the record with 45 T since 1999. Pulsed magnets go higher for a short time. The list of records of MagLab gives 100.75 T for one of its pulsed magnets.

The neodymium magnet dates from 1984. Two groups published the compound in the same issue of the Journal of Applied Physics: Masato Sagawa and colleagues, and John Croat and colleagues. According to Fischbacher and others, magnets based on it dominate the market for strong magnets.

07

What this means in practice

  • Shape and distance decide the pull. A thin disc is weak at its face, and every magnet loses its field with the cube of the distance (section 01). This is an inference of this page from the formulas above.
  • Heat is the enemy. The data sheet of N52 gives a Curie temperature of 310 °C, and the coercivity falls long before that (section 05). This page infers from the model of section 05 that a magnet which got too hot can stay weaker after it cools, because some of its grains have flipped.
  • The supply is a risk for Europe. On average, a hybrid or electric car contains 1.25 kg of strong magnets, and a wind turbine with direct drive needs 400 kg for each megawatt, according to Fischbacher and others. The EU gets 98 % of its rare-earth magnets from China, according to a briefing of the European Parliamentary Research Service of November 2025. Since 4 April 2025, China requires an export licence for seven rare earths and for magnets that contain them. A wider set of controls of October 2025 is suspended. On 9 November 2026, the Chinese Ministry of Commerce extended the suspension to 10 January 2027. Status of this point: 27 November 2026.
  • Hard disks write with heat. Sintered neodymium magnets move the arm of a hard disk, as the review of Fischbacher and others notes. The newest drives also use the effect of heat on the coercivity. Their method, HAMR, heats the spot under the head with a laser while the head writes. On 3 March 2026, Seagate announced that drives of this kind with up to 44 TB ship in volume to two cloud providers. The rest of this point is knowledge of the model: a disk stores each bit in the magnetisation of small grains, and a hot grain has a lower coercivity, so the head can flip grains that are stable when cold.
  • Holding costs nothing. A magnet that holds a note on a refrigerator does no work, because nothing moves. It needs no supply of energy, like a nail in a wall. This is an inference of this page.
08

Terms

Altermagnet
A material in which neighbouring spins cancel, as in an antiferromagnet, while the symmetry of its crystal still gives it some properties of a ferromagnet. Physicists have studied this class in the last few years. Wikipedia: Altermagnetism
Anisotropy field HK
The field that turns the magnetisation of an ideal grain out of its easy axis: HK=2K/μ0Ms. For Nd₂Fe₁₄B it corresponds to 7.65 T.
Antiferromagnet
A material in which the moments of neighbouring atoms point in opposite directions and cancel. It has no field outside. Wikipedia: Antiferromagnetism
Bohr magneton μB
The natural unit for the magnetic moment of an electron, 9.274 × 10−24 joule per tesla. It is built from the charge e of the electron, the reduced Planck constant ℏ and the mass me of the electron. Wikipedia: Bohr magneton
Boltzmann constant kB
The constant that turns a temperature into an energy: kBT is the typical thermal energy of one particle at the temperature T. At 20 °C it is 25 meV.
CODATA
The Committee on Data of the International Science Council. It publishes the recommended values of the constants of physics. The values on this page are those of 2022.
Coercivity
The opposing field that brings the magnetisation of a magnet to zero. A high coercivity means that the magnet is hard to demagnetise. Data sheets give it in ampere per metre. Wikipedia: Coercivity
Curie temperature TC
The temperature above which a ferromagnet loses its magnetic order. Iron has 1,043 K (770 °C). Wikipedia: Curie temperature
Dipole
The simplest source of a magnetic field: a small current loop, or a small magnet with a north and a south pole. Its field falls with the cube of the distance.
Domain, domain wall
A domain is a region in which all moments point the same way. A domain wall is the boundary between two domains. Inside the wall, the direction of the moments turns step by step from one side to the other. Wikipedia: Magnetic domain
eV (electronvolt), meV
A unit of energy: the energy that one electron gains across a voltage of 1 volt. 1 meV is a thousandth of it.
Exchange interaction, exchange energy J
The difference in electric energy between two electrons with parallel spins and two with opposite spins. It follows from the repulsion between electrons and the Pauli principle. J is its size for one pair of neighbours. Wikipedia: Exchange interaction
Ferrimagnet
A material in which neighbouring moments point in opposite directions but have unequal sizes, so a net moment remains. Wikipedia: Ferrimagnetism
Ferromagnet
A material in which the moments of neighbouring atoms align in the same direction without an outside field. Iron, cobalt and nickel are ferromagnets. Wikipedia: Ferromagnetism
HAMR (heat-assisted magnetic recording)
A method for hard disks. A laser heats the spot under the head while the head writes. Wikipedia: Heat-assisted magnetic recording
Hund’s rule
The electrons of a partly filled shell first take separate orbitals with parallel spins. The state with the largest total spin has the lowest energy. Friedrich Hund stated the rule in 1925. Wikipedia: Hund’s rules
Hysteresis
The state depends on the history and not only on the present input. For a magnet: at the same field, the magnetisation depends on the fields that were applied before. Wikipedia: Magnetic hysteresis
Ising model, spin si
A model of a magnet: each site i of a grid holds a spin si that is +1 or −1, and only neighbours interact. Wikipedia: Ising model
LHC (Large Hadron Collider)
The particle accelerator of the research centre CERN near Geneva, a ring of 27 km. Superconducting magnets bend the path of its protons. CERN: The Large Hadron Collider
Magnetic anisotropy, anisotropy constant K
The energy of a crystal depends on the direction of its magnetisation. The direction with the lowest energy is the easy axis. K is the energy per volume that it costs to turn the magnetisation away from it. Wikipedia: Magnetocrystalline anisotropy
Magnetic constant μ0
The constant that links a current to its magnetic field in SI units: 1.2566 × 10−6 tesla metre per ampere. A magnetisation or a field H in ampere per metre, multiplied by it, gives a value in tesla.
Magnetic field B, tesla (T)
The field says which force acts on a moving charge or on a magnet at each point of space. Its strength B has the unit tesla. 1 mT is a thousandth and 1 µT a millionth of a tesla. Wikipedia: Magnetic field
Magnetic moment
The strength and direction of a small magnet. For a current loop it is the current times the area of the loop. A field tries to turn a moment into its own direction. Wikipedia: Magnetic moment
Magnetisation M
The magnetic moment per volume of a material, in ampere per metre. Wikipedia: Magnetization
Metropolis algorithm
A method to draw random states with the probabilities of statistical mechanics. It proposes a small change, always accepts it if the energy falls, and accepts it with the probability e−ΔE/kBT if the energy rises by ΔE. Wikipedia: Metropolis–Hastings algorithm
MRI scanner (magnetic resonance imaging)
A medical scanner that makes pictures of the inside of the body with a strong magnetic field and radio waves. Wikipedia: Magnetic resonance imaging
Neodymium magnet, Nd₂Fe₁₄B, N52
A magnet made of the compound Nd₂Fe₁₄B: 2 atoms of neodymium and 1 atom of boron for 14 atoms of iron. N52 is one of the strongest common grades. Wikipedia: Neodymium magnet
NIST
The National Institute of Standards and Technology of the United States. It publishes reference data, among them the constants of physics and the spectra of atoms.
Orbital, shell, 3d and 4s
An orbital is one of the allowed wave shapes of an electron in an atom. It holds at most two electrons. A shell is a group of orbitals with similar energy. The 3d shell has five orbitals, and the 4s shell has one. Wikipedia: Atomic orbital
Paramagnet
A material whose atoms have magnetic moments that point in random directions. A field aligns them a little, and the effect ends with the field. Wikipedia: Paramagnetism
Pauli principle
No two electrons can be in the same state. In its general form: the wave function of two electrons changes its sign when the two swap. Wolfgang Pauli stated it in 1925. Wikipedia: Pauli exclusion principle
Rare earths, dysprosium
A group of 17 metallic elements. Neodymium and dysprosium belong to it. Dysprosium in a neodymium magnet raises the coercivity at high temperature. Wikipedia: Rare-earth element
Remanence Br
The field that a magnetised material keeps inside when no outside field acts and the field lines close without a gap. It is the usual measure for the strength of a magnet material. Wikipedia: Remanence
Saturation magnetisation Ms
The magnetisation of a material when all its moments are aligned. It is the highest value that the material can reach.
Sintered
Made from a fine powder that is pressed and then heated below its melting point until the grains bond. Wikipedia: Sintering
Spin
A fixed property of a particle that behaves like a rotation of fixed size. It makes the electron a small magnet. Measured along any axis, the spin of an electron has one of two values, called up and down. Wikipedia: Spin
Superconductor
A material that carries a current without resistance below a certain temperature. A coil made of it holds a strong field without a loss of power. Wikipedia: Superconductivity
Wave function
The description of a particle in quantum mechanics. Its square gives the probability to find the particle at each place. For two electrons it is one function of both positions. Wikipedia: Wave function
