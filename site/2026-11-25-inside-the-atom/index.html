What is an atom made of, down to the smallest parts?

Everything you can touch consists of atoms. An atom is a tiny, heavy nucleus inside a cloud of electrons. The nucleus consists of protons and neutrons, and each of these consists of three smaller particles called quarks. Inside quarks and electrons, experiments have found nothing so far. This page goes down through the four layers, shows what holds each one together, and says how and when each layer was found.

The structure

An atom has four layers: the atom, its nucleus, the protons and neutrons, and the quarks. The atom is up to 100,000 times wider than its nucleus. The nucleus still holds more than 99.9 % of the mass.

The parts

Three kinds of particles build every atom: electrons and two kinds of quarks, named up and down. Two more kinds of particles carry the forces that hold them together.

The limit

A measurement published in 2026 sees no structure inside quarks down to about 10−20 m. A proton is more than 100,000 times wider than that.
01

Four layers, each far smaller

An atom is about 10−10 m wide, a tenth of a nanometre. Its centre is the nucleus, which is 10,000 to 100,000 times smaller. If an atom were as wide as a football field, its nucleus would be about 1 mm wide. The nucleus still carries more than 99.9 % of the mass.

The nucleus is a cluster of protons and neutrons. A proton is 1.7 × 10−15 m wide. Each proton and each neutron contains three quarks. Quarks and electrons have no measured size: every experiment so far sees them as points.

A different force holds each layer together, and the binding gets stronger with depth. Physicists count the energies in electronvolts (eV): 1 eV is the energy that one electron gains across a voltage of 1 volt. Pulling the electron off a hydrogen atom costs 13.6 eV. Pulling a proton or a neutron out of a nucleus costs several million eV (MeV). A quark cannot be pulled out at all, as section 04 explains.

02

The electron is a standing wave

The nucleus is positive and the electrons are negative, so they attract each other. The first model of 1911 let the electrons circle the nucleus like planets. It could not be right: a circling charge radiates energy, so the electron would spiral into the nucleus.

Niels Bohr proposed in 1913 that only certain orbits are allowed. In 1926, Erwin Schrödinger found the equation behind this rule, and it treats the electron as a wave. A wave that is tied to a nucleus can take only certain shapes, like a guitar string that sounds only certain tones. Each shape is an orbital with a fixed energy. A measurement finds the electron more often where the wave is strong.

The lowest orbital is a ball around the nucleus. The electron cannot sink below it, because a wave that is squeezed into less space needs more energy of motion. This sets the size of the atom. For hydrogen, the energies of the orbitals follow a short rule:

En=−13.6eVn2

The number n = 1, 2, 3 and so on counts the shells, which are groups of orbitals with similar energy. The energy is negative because the electron is bound. When an electron drops to a lower orbital, the atom emits a photon, a particle of light, with exactly the energy difference. Each element therefore glows in its own sharp colours, and these spectral lines were the first test of the theory.

Atoms with several electrons need a second rule, which Wolfgang Pauli stated in 1925: no two electrons of an atom can be in the same state. One orbital holds two electrons, one for each value of a property called spin. The electrons fill the orbitals from the lowest energy upwards: 2 in shell 1, 8 in shell 2, 18 in shell 3. Only the outermost electrons form chemical bonds. Elements with the same number of outer electrons behave alike, and this is the pattern of the periodic table.

03

The nucleus holds the mass

The nucleus contains two kinds of particles with almost the same mass. A proton has one positive unit of charge, and a neutron has none. The number of protons decides the element: 1 for hydrogen, 6 for carbon, 79 for gold. A neutral atom has as many electrons as protons. Atoms of one element with different numbers of neutrons are its isotopes.

Particle masses are given as energies, through Einstein’s relation E=mc2 between energy, mass and the speed of light. A proton has 938.27 MeV, a neutron 939.57 MeV and an electron 0.511 MeV, in the 2026 values of the Particle Data Group. A proton is 1,836 times as heavy as an electron.

Protons repel each other electrically. A stronger force holds the nucleus together, the strong force. It reaches only about as far as a proton is wide, and it attracts protons and neutrons alike. Neutrons therefore add binding without repulsion, and heavy nuclei contain more neutrons than protons.

The binding shows up as missing mass. A carbon-12 atom weighs 92.2 MeV less than its 6 protons, 6 neutrons and 6 electrons weigh separately, as computed on this page from the 2026 values. This is 7.7 MeV for each particle in the nucleus, about 560,000 times the 13.6 eV of the electron in hydrogen.

A neutron outside a nucleus is not stable. After 878 seconds on average, it turns into a proton, an electron and an antineutrino. Inside a stable nucleus it stays, because the decay would cost energy there.

04

A proton is mostly energy

Protons and neutrons consist of two kinds of quarks. An up quark has the charge +2/3 and a down quark has −1/3, in units of the proton charge. A proton is two up quarks and one down quark, which gives +1. A neutron is one up quark and two down quarks, which gives 0.

Quarks carry a second kind of charge. It has the name colour, although it has nothing to do with visible colours. The strong force acts between colour charges, and its carriers are the gluons. Photons carry the electric force and have no electric charge themselves. Gluons do carry colour, so they pull on each other. The field between two quarks therefore forms a narrow tube, and its pull does not fade with distance.

The result is confinement: nobody has observed a single free quark. When a collision knocks a quark out of a proton, the energy in the stretching tube turns into new quarks and antiquarks. A narrow spray of ordinary particles leaves the collision, a jet. The force that binds a nucleus is the remainder of the strong force outside the protons and neutrons.

The masses of the quarks explain almost nothing of the proton mass. An up quark has about 2.2 MeV and a down quark about 4.7 MeV. The three quarks of a proton sum to 9 MeV, which is 1 % of its 938 MeV. The rest is energy: the motion of the quarks and the energy of the gluon field count as mass through E=mc2. A calculation of 2018 on supercomputers, with a method called lattice QCD, splits the proton mass into four parts.

05

The full list has 17 particles

The theory of these particles and forces is the Standard Model, which took its form in the early 1970s. It lists 17 kinds of elementary particles: 6 quarks, 6 leptons, 4 carriers of forces and the Higgs boson.

Atoms use only the first column of the chart: the up quark, the down quark and the electron. Its fourth member, the neutrino, appears in radioactive decays. Nature repeats this set of four twice with higher masses. The particles of these second and third generations decay within a fraction of a second. The muon, a heavier copy of the electron, lives 2.2 microseconds on average. Why there are three generations is an open question.

Three forces act between the particles, each through a carrier: the photon for the electromagnetic force, the gluon for the strong force, and the W and Z bosons for the weak force. The weak force is the only one that turns one kind of particle into another. In the decay of the neutron, it turns a down quark into an up quark. Gravity is not part of the Standard Model.

The last entry is the Higgs boson, found in 2012. It belongs to a field that fills all space and gives the elementary particles their mass. The masses differ widely. An electron has 0.511 MeV. A top quark has 172,600 MeV, about as much as a whole atom of tungsten.

06

How each layer was found

Nobody can look at these layers. Every step down used the same method, scattering: shoot fast particles at a target and count how many fly off at which angle. A soft, spread-out target deflects them a little. A small, hard centre sometimes throws one far off its path.

In 1909, Hans Geiger and Ernest Marsden shot alpha particles at a thin gold foil, and about one in a few thousand bounced back. Ernest Rutherford concluded in 1911 that the positive charge of an atom sits in a tiny centre. From 1967 on, a team at the Stanford Linear Accelerator Center in California repeated the idea one layer deeper, with electrons of 4 to 21 GeV. Protons threw more electrons to large angles than a soft ball could, so they contain hard grains.

The depth that a probe reaches depends on its energy. A particle is also a wave, and a wave cannot show details that are much smaller than its wavelength. The wavelength shrinks as the momentum grows, a relation that Louis de Broglie proposed in the 1920s. For a particle near the speed of light, the smallest detail that it resolves is about:

d≈ℏcE

Here E is the energy of the particle, and ℏc, a product of two constants of nature, is 197 MeV × 10−15 m. Each factor of ten in depth costs a factor of ten in energy. This is the reason for ever larger accelerators. The LHC at CERN near Geneva is a ring of 27 km, and it collided protons at 13.6 TeV.

07

What is below, and what is missing

Nobody knows whether quarks and electrons are the last layer. No experiment has found anything inside them. In 2016, the ZEUS group published an upper limit for the radius of a quark, from data of the collider HERA in Hamburg: 0.43 × 10−18 m. In April 2026, CERN reported a result of CMS, one of the experiments at the LHC. CMS compared the angles between pairs of jets with the prediction for point-like quarks. It found no significant difference, down to about 10−20 m. Searches for common inner parts of electrons and quarks found none either.

“Point-like” means smaller than the resolution of the best experiment. A size of zero is not proven.

Ideas for a deeper layer exist, for example string theory, which replaces the points with tiny vibrating strings. No experiment has confirmed one. This paragraph rests on the general knowledge of the model that wrote this page.

The list of particles is incomplete in other ways. The Standard Model does not describe gravity. It treats neutrinos as massless, and measurements show that they have a small mass. And atoms are not all the matter that exists. In 2013, the Royal Swedish Academy of Sciences wrote that visible matter is only one fifth of all matter in the cosmos. The rest is called dark matter, and none of the 17 particles fits it.

The next step in depth will take time. The LHC stopped on 27 June 2026 for an upgrade of four years, which aims at about ten times more collision data.

08

What this means in practice

For daily work, nothing changes: no software and no device depends on what is inside a quark. The upper layers matter. Chemistry is the outer electrons of section 02, which rearrange at a few eV. Transistors, LEDs and flash memory are built on the same wave behaviour of electrons. Nuclear energy comes from one layer deeper, where the binding is about 560,000 times stronger. This is why a nuclear fuel stores far more energy per kilogram than a chemical one. This section is an inference of this page and rests on the general knowledge of the model.

09

Terms

Nucleus
The centre of an atom. It consists of protons and neutrons, has a positive electric charge and holds more than 99.9 % of the mass of the atom.
Electron
A light elementary particle with one negative unit of electric charge. The electrons of an atom form a cloud around the nucleus and make its chemical bonds.
Proton
A particle of the nucleus with one positive unit of electric charge. It consists of two up quarks and one down quark. The number of protons decides which element an atom is.
Neutron
A particle of the nucleus without electric charge, slightly heavier than a proton. It consists of one up quark and two down quarks.
Quark
An elementary particle that feels the strong force. Six kinds exist: up, down, charm, strange, top and bottom. Quarks occur only in groups, for example three in a proton. Wikipedia: Quark
Gluon
The carrier particle of the strong force. Gluons bind the quarks inside protons and neutrons. They have no mass.
Photon
The particle of light, and the carrier particle of the electromagnetic force. It has no mass and no electric charge.
eV (electronvolt), keV, MeV, GeV, TeV
A unit of energy: the energy that one electron gains when it crosses a voltage of 1 volt, 1.6 × 10−19 joule. The prefixes mean a thousand (keV), a million (MeV), a billion (GeV) and a million million (TeV) electronvolts. Particle masses are also given in these units.
Orbital
One of the allowed wave shapes of an electron in an atom. It says where the electron is likely to be found, and it has a fixed energy. An orbital holds at most two electrons. Wikipedia: Atomic orbital
Shell
A group of orbitals with the same number n and similar energy. Shell 1 holds 2 electrons, shell 2 holds 8 and shell 3 holds 18.
n (shell number)
A whole number from 1 upwards that counts the shells of an atom. Physicists call it the principal quantum number. A larger n means a larger orbital and a weaker binding.
En (energy of an orbital)
The energy of the electron of a hydrogen atom in shell n. Zero means a free electron at rest, so a bound electron has a negative energy.
Spin
A property of a particle that behaves like a rotation of fixed size. For an electron it has two possible values, often called up and down.
Isotope
Atoms of one element with the same number of protons and different numbers of neutrons. Carbon-12 has 6 neutrons and carbon-14 has 8.
Strong force
The force between particles that carry colour charge: quarks and gluons. It binds quarks into protons and neutrons, and its remainder outside these particles binds them into nuclei.
Weak force
A force with a very short range, carried by the W and Z bosons. It can turn one kind of quark or lepton into another, and it causes one kind of radioactive decay.
Colour (colour charge)
The charge of the strong force. It exists in three variants. The name is a label and has nothing to do with light.
Confinement
The fact that quarks and gluons never occur alone. They are always bound in particles such as protons, because the strong force does not get weaker with distance.
Jet
A narrow spray of particles that leaves a collision in one direction. It forms when a quark or a gluon is knocked out with high energy, and it shows the direction and the energy of that quark or gluon.
Lattice QCD
A method that computes the strong force numerically on a grid of points in space and time. QCD stands for quantum chromodynamics, the theory of the strong force. The calculations run on supercomputers.
Standard Model
The theory of the known elementary particles and of three forces between them: the electromagnetic, the strong and the weak force. CERN: The Standard Model
Lepton
An elementary particle of matter that does not feel the strong force. The six leptons are the electron, the muon, the tau and one neutrino for each of them.
Neutrino
A lepton without electric charge and with a very small mass, below 0.45 eV according to the Particle Data Group 2026. It feels only the weak force and gravity, so it passes through matter almost without effect.
Generation
One of three sets of four matter particles: two quarks, one charged lepton and one neutrino. The sets differ in mass. The first generation is the up quark, the down quark, the electron and the electron neutrino.
Higgs boson
The particle of the Higgs field, a field that fills all space and gives the elementary particles their mass. It was found at CERN in 2012 and has a mass of 125 GeV. Boson is the name of a class of particles that includes all carriers of forces.
Antiparticle
The partner of a matter particle, with the same mass and the opposite charge. Every quark and every lepton has one: the antiquark, the antineutrino, and for the electron the positron.
Scattering
An experiment in which particles are shot at a target. The number of particles that leave at each angle shows how the charge or the matter in the target is distributed.
Alpha particle
The nucleus of a helium atom: two protons and two neutrons. Some radioactive elements emit alpha particles at high speed.
d (smallest detail)
The smallest length that a probe of a given energy can resolve, as an order of magnitude.
ℏ (reduced Planck constant)
The constant of nature that links the energy of a wave to its frequency, and the momentum of a particle to its wavelength. It is the Planck constant divided by 2π.
c (speed of light)
299,792,458 metres per second.
E (energy of the probe)
The energy of the particle that an experiment shoots at its target. The formula holds for particles near the speed of light.
CERN
The European laboratory for particle physics, near Geneva. It runs the LHC. home.cern
DESY (Deutsches Elektronen-Synchrotron)
A German research centre for accelerators in Hamburg. Its colliders PETRA and HERA found the gluon and measured the inside of the proton. desy.de
HERA and ZEUS
HERA was a collider at DESY in Hamburg that shot electrons at protons. ZEUS was one of its detectors, run by a group of the same name.
LHC (Large Hadron Collider)
The accelerator of CERN near Geneva, a ring of 27 km. From 2022 to 2026 it collided protons with a total energy of 13.6 TeV. CERN: The Large Hadron Collider
CMS (Compact Muon Solenoid)
One of the two large general detectors at the LHC, run by a collaboration of several thousand scientists. cms.cern
