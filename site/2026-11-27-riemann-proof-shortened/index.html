Mathematics · status of 27 November 2026

How a 17-million-line proof shrank to 113,000 lines

The Riemann Hypothesis is a statement from 1859 about the zeros of one function. It fixes how evenly the prime numbers are spread. On 20 November 2026 Levent Alpöge published a proof that he found with the AI Claude: 17.4 million lines for the proof checker Lean. Five days later a team at Anthropic published the same theorems in 112,827 lines. This page explains what was cut and how, which checks each version has passed, and what the two versions teach. The problem itself is the subject of an earlier page.

Status: three versions that programs accept, none that a person has reviewed The last check of the original proof finished on 25 November 2026 at 21:49 UTC. The checks of the two short editions finished on 26 November. No mathematician has read one of the three versions to the end, and the Clay institute lists the problem as “Active”, not as solved (read on 27 November).

What shrank

The proof, by a factor of 154. The five theorems that it proves kept their exact wording.

How

Largely by simpler mathematics for the steps in between, not only by tidying. Anthropic has not published the method, the model or the cost.

What it teaches

A statement that a program can check makes a complete rewrite safe. Reading by people stays the slow part.
01

The same theorems in 1/154 of the text

In Lean, a theorem is a type and a proof is a program of that type. Checking the proof is type checking. A small trusted core, the kernel, does this, and it does not need to understand the mathematics. The proofs here build on Mathlib, the mathematics library of the Lean community.

Both the original and the short proof have the same shape as a software project with a small public header. The file Statements.lean has 16 lines and imports only Mathlib. It states what is claimed. Main.lean proves five theorems of these types. All other files are implementation.

-- from Statements.lean (16 lines)
def RiemannZetaZerosOnLine : Prop :=
  ∀ s : ℂ, riemannZeta s = 0 →
    (¬∃ n : ℕ,
        s = -2 * ((n : ℂ) + 1)) →
    s ≠ 1 → s.re = 1 / 2

-- from Main.lean, 1 of 5 theorems
theorem riemannHypothesis :
    RiemannHypothesis :=
  New.zeta_zeros_on_line New.grh

The definition reads: for every complex number s, if the zeta function is zero at s, and s is none of −2, −4, −6, … and not 1, then the real part of s is 1/2. RiemannHypothesis is Mathlib’s own definition of the same statement. The other three theorems state the Generalized Riemann Hypothesis for one family of similar functions.

On 25 November 2026 at about 20:40 UTC, Luke Sernau of Anthropic published the repository lsernau/grh-lean. It holds two new proofs of the same five theorems: a “readable” edition and a “compact” edition. The chart compares them with the original.

The short repository counts differently, and both numbers are in use: about 76,000 lines of code for the compact edition and about 300,000 for the readable one, without comments and blank lines. With them, the files have 112,827 and 477,124 lines.

The view “Export” shows a detail that the line count hides. An export holds the proof after Lean has expanded all automation into explicit terms, together with everything it needs from Mathlib. The compact edition has less source text than the readable one, but its export is larger: 5.17 GB against 4.49 GB. Andrew Sutherland of the university MIT gives the reason: the compact edition has fewer declarations, and they have larger proof terms on average. Short source that leans on automation can expand into more work for the checker, as a short template can expand into a large binary.

02

How the proof was shortened

The size of the original was no secret. In his note of 20 November, Alpöge wrote that he may have built “strange suboptimal choices” into the argument and that he expected short arguments to follow. They followed in five days:

  1. 20 NovAlpöge publishes the proof: 175,187 Lean files with 17,374,215 lines. The Lean community opens a repository to clean it up.
  2. 21 NovThe Lean and Mathlib communities certify the statement and the proof.
  3. 24 NovThree mathematicians announce a Polymath project on the blog of Terence Tao. Its aim is a proof that people can read.
  4. 25 Nov, about 20:40 UTCSernau publishes the two short editions.
  5. 25 Nov, 22:24 UTCThe community repository moves its main branch to the readable edition. The work on the original stays on a branch.
  6. 26 NovIndependent checks of both short editions finish. A first text of the compact edition for people appears, written by AI.

Why the first version was so long

Three observations come from the discussion of the Lean community on 23 November.

  • No library was built. Mario Carneiro wrote that the AI systems of the labs go for the goal at all costs, and that 90 % of proving a big result is building a library. He predicted that the proof could shrink by 98 % or more.
  • Dead code. The number theorist David Loeffler found a structure that states a large theorem, the full local Langlands correspondence. About 6,000 files import it. Nothing ever proves it, and nothing needs it: the proof seems to use only a much simpler special case.
  • Many small files. A profile of one build of the community repository counted 103 CPU hours, about half of them for loading imports. This page infers the average of the original from its two totals: 99 lines per file.

What Sernau says about the method

Sernau announced the short proof in the forum of the Lean community. He wrote that he had worked on it with a few colleagues for some days, and that he is not able to say much about the methods. The README of the repository adds that Claude wrote the Lean text and the documents. He named three things:

  • Unroll, simplify, roll up. He described “unrolling definitions to get a long, flat proof”, looking for simplifications there, and rolling the result up again. This lets a simplification cross the boundary between two definitions. A compiler does the same when it inlines functions before it optimises. The comparison stops at one point: a compiler must keep the behaviour of the program, and here only the final theorems had to stay.
  • New arguments. Sernau wrote that much of the gain came from simpler proofs of results in between, so many of the arguments are new. Whole theories left the proof. Section 03 lists them.
  • Golf first, then clarity. The team first reduced the proof to the compact edition and then rewrote it for clarity. So the readable edition is the later one. It also states some results that the five theorems do not use, for people who want to write the proof as a text.

The repository says of itself that it follows the method of Alpöge’s proof, that many steps are proved by other arguments, and that parts were ported from the original and rewritten, mostly background material.

Not public: the Claude model, the tools around it, the computing power and the prompts. A maintainer of the community repository, Thomas Murrills, thanked Sernau and regretted this point: tools for cleaning up such proofs would help the whole community.

03

What is inside the short proof

Each edition has two parts. Lib holds background that the authors class as known in the literature and that Mathlib lacks. New holds the argument itself. Lib imports nothing from New. The original had no such border. In the compact edition, Naskręcki counts 24,850 lines of code with 1,571 theorems in New and 51,280 lines of code in Lib. So two thirds of the short proof are library work that the proof needs and Mathlib does not have.

The names are those of the repository. The smallest folder of the background, Analytic, holds the classical analysis of prime numbers. The large folders belong to algebra and geometry: representations, cohomology, Shimura varieties. A statement about the zeros of an analytic function is proved here mostly with tools from other fields.

The guide of the readable edition warns that Lib is no general library. It proves parts of standard theories, “often in a weaker form that suffices here” and with its own definitions.

The argument in one inequality

The folder Endgame and the file New/Main.lean finish the proof, in 684 lines together. The central lemma has a short statement:

lemma star_inequality
    {P} {D : Data P} :
    D.m * D.remainder
      ≤ D.logB ^ 2 * D.budget
  • remainder is a weighted count of primes up to a bound x, minus its expected value. The Riemann Hypothesis follows if this error is small. A theorem of Edmund Landau from 1905 shows that a bound from above is enough.
  • m is the number of theta lifts that the proof chains. The source sets it between x and 2x.
  • The right side is a budget for all errors of the construction.

Divide by m: the error of the count is at most the budget divided by about x. A comment in the source says it in one phrase: the saving x^(-1/2) is 1/m.

The inequality comes from one quantity that the proof bounds from two sides: the covolume of a lattice of theta lifts. Theorems A and B of the source bound it from above. Theorem C bounds it from below. A large main amount appears in both bounds and cancels. The remainder is what is left.

What was cut

Sernau lists what the short proof no longer needs. This page does not explain these theories. Each of them was a dependency of the original.

  • The dependence on a weak form of Vandiver’s conjecture. The original uses no unproved assumption, so it had to prove this form itself. The earlier page describes this step, which goes beyond the published mathematics.
  • Resolution of singularities, a large theorem of algebraic geometry from 1964.
  • The local Langlands correspondence, patching, Hida theory and the group GSp4, all from modern number theory.
  • The “slope inequality” of the original. A product formula for one determinant replaces it.

One limit of the border between known and new: the authors call it a working classification. The compact edition lists 196 citations. For 57 of them, the cited place was compared with the source and confirmed. For 102, only the work was found.

04

Which checks each version passed

A check by a program can fail in two ways: the files prove another statement than the one intended, or they assume something without proof. The table shows what covers these two risks for each version, and what covers the third risk, that nobody understands the result.

The wrong statement. Comparator takes a challenge file that states the five theorems with sorry in place of the proofs. It checks that the solution proves exactly these statements with the permitted axioms, and then runs the kernels. Sutherland reports that Statements.lean is the same file, byte for byte, in the two short editions, and that its four definitions equal those of the original as text.

One difference stays. The original builds on Mathlib of 21 August 2026, the short editions on Mathlib of 13 October 2026. Sutherland compared the two challenge files. The definitions at the top, such as riemannZeta and RiemannHypothesis, are the same terms. Of 50,232 declarations below them with the same name, 10,750 differ in some respect. He writes that he has not checked formally that the two versions are equivalent.

The hidden assumption. A text search of both short editions finds no declared axiom, no sorry outside the challenge file and no code that runs during the build. Sutherland calls this search a precaution. The proof of this point is the list of axioms and the kernel runs.

The bound of Schoenfeld. A second project derives a classical bound from the compact edition. Take the count of primes up to x in a weighted form. For every x from 73.2 on, it differs from x by less than:

x⋅(ln⁡x)28π

The statement uses only primes, a square root, a logarithm and the number π, so it does not depend on how Mathlib defines the zeta function. Lowell Schoenfeld showed in 1976 that the Riemann Hypothesis implies this bound. The combined proof passed Comparator on 26 November. AI agents wrote that project too.

What no program checks. The README of the short proof says it plainly: the proof has passed machine checks only, no mathematician has read it through, and no paper comes with it. Naskręcki published a text of the compact edition on 26 November. AI agents wrote it, other AI agents reviewed it, and its own README says that no person has refereed it. Kim Morrison, who moved the community repository to the short proof, wrote on 27 November about the many AI translations of this kind that there is “zero evidence that anyone has read any of them”.

05

What the two versions teach

17.4 million lines

From the original

Correct is not tidy
The kernel accepted a proof with dead code and with detours that nobody had measured.
The mess costs
Lean’s kernel needed 48 hours for the export, and about half an hour for each short edition.
It holds more mathematics
Some results on the way are new, and the short proof dropped them.
113,000 lines

From the short version

What was not needed
Several heavy theories, and one step beyond the published mathematics.
Where the new part is
In about 25,000 lines of code, apart from the background.
Lines are a weak measure
Fewer lines gave a larger export, and a line count can be gamed.

From the original

The long version is not obsolete. Somebody asked what would have been lost if Anthropic had published only the short proof. Loeffler answered on 26 November: significant new mathematics. His example is a blog post by the number theorist Frank Calegari about an intermediate result of the original, connected with Vandiver’s conjecture. The short proof no longer contains it. Calegari writes that a part can be interesting even if the final proof does not need it. He also writes that he has found no precise statement of that result yet, only texts that AI made from the Lean files.

Dead code hides the dependencies. The unused Langlands structure of section 02 made the proof look as if it rested on one of the hardest recent theorems. It did not. For mathematicians, the list of what a proof needs is a result of its own.

From the short version

Fewer dependencies open new questions. In the forum of the Polymath project, Loeffler notes that the short proof needs no form of Vandiver’s conjecture and seems to need a second tool, Kato’s Euler system, no longer. He calls these the two largest obstacles to extending the proof to a wider class of number fields.

The compact edition is near a floor for mechanical shortening. Vincent Zhang reported an experiment in the same forum on 27 November. An AI agent tried to shorten the compact edition and to keep it valid. Result: from 76,206 to 76,199 lines of code. Replacing proof blocks by stronger automation worked in 0 of 370 attempts, and he found no dead code. A public variant with “53,691 lines” turned out to have renamed 841 declarations to two or three characters. The count of identifier tokens is almost the same in both: 710,957 and 710,651. This is one report by one person, and nobody has repeated it.

One definition can set the cost of a check. The checker lean4lean accepts the compact export in 29 minutes with 7 GB of memory. For the readable export it needs 2 hours 35 minutes and 332 GB. Of this time, 2 hours 12 minutes go to one definition, tiltTheory, which the compact edition defines in another way. Sernau found that moving one hypothesis into its own lemma removes the problem, and wrote that he does not know why.

From the week between them

A fixed statement makes a rewrite safe. This page infers it from the timeline. The community moved its main branch from its cleaned copy of the original to 477,000 lines by other authors in less than two hours. This works because the same 16 lines of statements and the same Comparator run decide what counts. No review of the new text was needed for that decision.

The rewrite overtook the cleanup. For five days, many contributors with AI agents had removed code from the original, pull request by pull request. The repository had 541 forks on 27 November. Some of them wrote that the short proof overtook this work. Daniel Ang asked whether a complete rethink can be crowdsourced at all, or needs a few people with much computing power.

The target was changed at once. One reader called the compact edition “reward hacking for fewer lines”. The community chose the readable edition, which is four times longer, as its base. Loeffler asked the maintainers to stop merging changes that save lines at the cost of readability.

06

What this means in practice

Nothing changes for the result. Programs accept the theorem in three forms, and mathematics as a community has accepted none of them. For encryption and daily life, the earlier page gives the answer: no effect.

For work with AI agents on software, this page draws three inferences. No source states them.

  • The first working version of an agent can be far from the smallest one. Here the factor was 154, and a second pass found it in days. The second pass was cheap to trust because a machine checks the specification completely. A test suite checks samples. The same rewrite of a program needs more care.
  • Measure what you want. Lines of code fell while the export grew, and a renaming pass produced a “shorter” proof with the same number of tokens. An agent that is rewarded for a number will move the number.
  • Review is the scarce resource. In the week after the announcement, AI produced two shorter proofs, a text of 1,356 pages and many summaries. The number of people who can judge the mathematics did not change. Naskręcki, quoted by heise online, sees the limit no longer in the formal proof, but in the reading by people.
07

Terms

Riemann Hypothesis
The conjecture of Bernhard Riemann from 1859: every zero of the zeta function, apart from the known ones at −2, −4, …, has the real part 1/2. It is equivalent to a sharp bound on how far the count of primes can differ from its prediction. The earlier page explains it
Zeta function (Riemann zeta function, ζ)
A function of one complex number. Where the real part of its argument is above 1, it is the sum of the powers of 1/n with this argument as exponent, over all whole numbers n from 1 on. Its zeros control the distribution of the primes. Wikipedia
Generalized Riemann Hypothesis (GRH)
The same statement for a family of functions similar to zeta. The proofs here cover the Dirichlet L-functions, which describe primes in arithmetic progressions such as 3, 7, 11, 19, 23, … Wikipedia
Lean
A programming language and proof checker. A mathematical statement is a type, and a proof is a program of that type. lean-lang.org
Mathlib
The mathematics library of the Lean community, with the definitions that the theorems here use: complex numbers, the zeta function, Dirichlet characters. leanprover-community.github.io
Kernel
The small part of Lean that type checks finished proof terms. Everything else in Lean, such as automation, only produces terms for the kernel. A bug outside the kernel cannot make a false theorem pass.
The three standard axioms
Statements that Lean accepts without proof and that almost all of Mathlib uses: propext, Quot.sound and Classical.choice, the axiom of choice. A proof that needs a fourth axiom assumes something more.
sorry
A Lean keyword that stands in for a missing proof. Lean accepts the file with a warning, and the theorem then depends on an extra axiom. A finished proof contains none.
Export
A file with all declarations that a theorem depends on, as fully explicit terms, Mathlib included. Checkers other than Lean read this format.
Independent checkers
Kernels written again from scratch by other people: nanoda in Rust, lean4lean in Lean itself, con-leche and its Rust port con-ron. A bug in Lean’s kernel is unlikely to exist in all of them.
Comparator
A tool of the Lean project. It compares the statements of a solution with a challenge file, checks the list of axioms, and runs Lean’s kernel and the independent checkers on the export. github.com/leanprover/comparator
Theta lift
A method from the theory of automorphic forms. It turns a highly symmetric function for one group of symmetries into one for another group. The proof applies it many times in a chain: about the square root of the bound up to which it counts primes. Wikipedia
Covolume of a lattice
A lattice is a regular grid of points in a space of many dimensions. Its covolume is the volume of one cell of the grid. A grid of short vectors has a small covolume.
Shimura varieties
Geometric spaces whose points carry arithmetic information. Modern number theory uses them to connect symmetric functions with equations in whole numbers. Wikipedia
Vandiver’s conjecture
An open question of number theory, named after Harry Vandiver. It says that a prime p never divides a certain class number that belongs to the p-th roots of 1. Wikipedia
Polymath project
An open collaboration of many mathematicians on one problem, in public forums. Results appear under a shared pseudonym. The announcement
MIT
Massachusetts Institute of Technology, a university in Cambridge near Boston, USA.
UTC
Coordinated Universal Time. Austria is one hour ahead of it in winter.
